## 1.1 Introduction
Look around you. Why does a ball thrown up always come back down? Why does milk turn into curd overnight? Why does the Moon look like a thin silver line on some nights and a full round plate on others? Why do the leaves of a touch-me-not plant fold up when you touch them?

You ask questions like these every day. Each time you wonder "**why?**", "**how?**" or "**what if?**", you are thinking like a scientist. Science begins with **curiosity** – the wish to know and understand.

SAY: riya-think | Is science only done by people in white coats, inside big laboratories?
SAY: buddy | Not at all, Riya! Science happens in kitchens, gardens, playgrounds and fields – anywhere someone asks a question and looks carefully for the answer.

Science is not just a collection of facts written in books. It is a **way of finding out** about the world – by looking closely, asking questions, making careful guesses, testing them, and accepting only what the evidence shows. Anybody can do it, and everybody uses its results: the light bulb in your room, the medicine that cures a fever, the phone in your family's hands and the bus that brings you to school all exist because of science.

> **Definition: Science**
> **Science** is a way of learning about the natural world by **observing**, **asking questions**, **testing ideas** through experiments, and drawing conclusions based on **evidence**.

In this chapter, you will learn how scientists work, what the main branches of science are, which tools help us observe, and how scientists – including many from India – have changed the way we live.

## 1.2 Science Begins with Observation
To **observe** means to notice things carefully using our **senses** – sight, hearing, touch, smell and taste. A scientist observes not just once, but again and again, and writes down what is seen.

> **Activity 1.1: Be a careful observer**
> **You need:** a leaf, a stone, a hand lens (magnifying glass) and your notebook.
> **What to do:** Look at the leaf with your eyes first and write down three things you notice. Then look at it through the hand lens. Do the same with the stone.
> **Think:** Did you notice new things through the lens – tiny veins, hairs or shiny grains? How many more observations could you write?

Good observations are **careful**, **honest** and **recorded**. Writing them down helps us compare and remember. Sometimes we need **tools** to observe things our senses cannot catch – things that are too small, too far, too hot or too cold.

### Tools that help us observe
| Tool | What it helps us do |
| Hand lens (magnifying glass) | See small things in more detail |
| Microscope | See very tiny things, such as cells, that we cannot see with our eyes alone |
| Telescope | See faraway objects like the Moon, planets and stars |
| Thermometer | Measure how hot or cold something is |
| Measuring scale and tape | Measure length |
| Weighing balance | Measure mass |
| Measuring cylinder | Measure the volume of a liquid |
| Stopwatch | Measure time |

> **Did you know?**
> Our senses can sometimes be fooled! Put one hand in cold water and the other in warm water for a minute. Then dip both hands in the same bowl of normal water. One hand feels it is warm and the other feels it is cold! That is why scientists use measuring tools such as thermometers instead of relying only on feelings.

## 1.3 How Scientists Work: The Scientific Method
Scientists follow a set of steps to find reliable answers. These steps are together called the **scientific method**. Let us follow Aarav as he uses it.

SAY: aarav-think | The money plant on our window always bends towards the glass. Is it trying to look outside?
SAY: buddy | That's a perfect observation to start with, Aarav! Let's use the scientific method to find out why.

**Step 1 – Observe:** Aarav notices that the plant near the window grows bent towards the window.
**Step 2 – Ask a question:** Why does the plant bend towards the window?
**Step 3 – Make a hypothesis:** A **hypothesis** is a sensible guess that can be tested. Aarav guesses: "Plants grow towards light."
**Step 4 – Test it with an experiment:** He takes two similar potted plants. He places one inside a cardboard box with a hole cut in one side. The other is kept in the open, with light all around it. Both get the same soil, the same water and the same pot.
**Step 5 – Observe and record the results:** After a week, the plant in the box has bent towards the hole. The plant in the open has grown straight up.
**Step 6 – Conclude:** The evidence supports his hypothesis – plants grow towards light.
**Step 7 – Share and ask more questions:** Aarav tells his class. Riya asks: "Will it happen with every plant? With coloured light?" New questions start the method all over again!

### A fair test
In Aarav's experiment, only **one** thing was different between the two plants – the light. Everything else was kept the same. This is called a **fair test**.

> **Definition: Fair test and variable**
> Anything that can change in an experiment is called a **variable**. In a **fair test**, we change **only one variable** at a time and keep all the others the same, so that we know exactly what caused the result.

> **Example 1.1**
> Riya wants to find out whether seeds sprout faster in warm or in cold places. She keeps 10 moong seeds on wet cotton near the warm kitchen window and 10 bean seeds on dry cotton in the cold storeroom. Is this a fair test?
> **Solution:** No. She has changed three things – the place (warm/cold), the kind of seed (moong/bean) and the water (wet/dry). She should use the **same kind of seed** and the **same wet cotton** in both, and change **only the temperature**.

SAY: riya-wow | So if I change too many things together, I can never know which one made the difference!

### Why scientists repeat experiments
A single result can happen by chance. Scientists **repeat** their experiments many times, and other scientists repeat them too. If everyone gets the same result, we can trust it. If new evidence shows an idea is wrong, scientists change their idea. This honesty is what makes science powerful.

> **Remember:** In science, we believe an idea because of **evidence**, not because many people say it or because it is written somewhere.

## 1.4 Branches of Science
The world is so vast that science is divided into different **branches**, each studying one part of it.

| Branch | What it studies | Example of a question it asks |
| Biology | Living things – plants, animals, the human body | How does food give us energy? |
| Physics | Matter, motion, force, energy, light, sound, electricity | Why does a ball roll down a slope? |
| Chemistry | Materials, what they are made of and how they change | Why does iron rust? |
| Earth science | Rocks, soil, water, weather and the Earth itself | What causes earthquakes? |
| Astronomy | Stars, planets, the Moon and outer space | Why does the Moon change shape? |

FIG: c6-sci-1-branches.svg | Fig. 1.4: The main branches of science and some things they study

The branches are connected. For example, a doctor uses biology (the body), chemistry (medicines) and physics (X-ray machines) to treat a patient. Many of today's most exciting discoveries happen where two branches meet.

## 1.5 Science All Around Us
Science is part of everything we do, even when we do not notice it.
- **In the kitchen:** cooking changes raw food; sugar dissolves in tea; a pressure cooker cooks food faster by trapping steam.
- **On the playground:** a swing moves back and forth; a ball bounces; a bicycle stays balanced while moving.
- **In nature:** a rainbow forms when sunlight splits into colours; seeds sprout after rain; birds fly south in winter.
- **In our health:** vaccines protect us from diseases; clean water and soap keep germs away.
- **In technology:** mobile phones, computers, electricity and satellites that bring us weather reports and maps.

### Science and our responsibility
Science gives us great power, and we must use it wisely. Plastics made life easier, but they also pollute rivers and oceans. Fuels run our vehicles, but their smoke dirties the air. A good scientist – and a good citizen – thinks about how discoveries affect people, animals and the planet.

- The **scientific method**: observe → ask a question → make a hypothesis → experiment → record results → conclude → share and ask new questions.
- A **hypothesis** is a sensible guess that can be tested.
- In a **fair test**, only **one variable** is changed at a time.
- Experiments are **repeated** to make sure results are reliable.

Take a slow walk around your school or your neighbourhood. Living things differ from one another in size, shape, colour, the places where they live and the food they eat. Yet they are also similar in many ways. Studying these **similarities and differences** helps us group living things and understand them better.

> **Definition: Diversity and biodiversity**
> **Diversity** means **variety**. **Biodiversity** is the variety of all the different kinds of plants, animals and other living things found in an area.

In this chapter, we will learn how plants can be grouped by their stems, leaves, roots and seeds, how animals are suited to the places where they live, and why biodiversity is precious.

## 2.2 Grouping Living Things
Imagine a library where the books are placed on shelves at random. Finding one book would take hours! Libraries **group** books – stories on one shelf, science on another. In the same way, scientists group living things based on their features. Grouping makes it easier to study, compare and remember them.

> **Activity 2.1: Sort and group**
> **You need:** pictures or real samples of 12 plants and animals (for example, mango, grass, rose, money plant, pumpkin, sparrow, fish, cow, butterfly, frog, earthworm, snake).
> **What to do:** Sort them into groups. You can choose any feature – where they live, how they move, what they eat, whether they have legs.
> **Think:** Did your friends make the same groups? Is there more than one correct way to group living things?

You will find that there are many ways to group. Scientists choose features that are clear and easy to check.

## 2.3 Grouping Plants by their Stems
Look at the **stem** of a plant. Is it soft and green, or hard and woody? Is it thin and weak, or thick and strong? Based on their stems and height, plants are grouped as herbs, shrubs and trees. Plants with weak stems are called climbers or creepers.

| Group | Height | Stem | Examples |
| Herbs | Short, usually less than 1 metre | Soft, green, tender | Tulsi, mint, coriander, wheat, grass |
| Shrubs | Medium height | Hard and woody; many branches start near the base | Rose, hibiscus, lemon, jasmine |
| Trees | Tall | Thick, hard, woody **trunk**; branches start high above the ground | Mango, neem, banyan, peepal |
| Climbers | Grow upwards with support | Weak stem that climbs using tendrils or by twining | Money plant, grapevine, pea, bitter gourd |
| Creepers | Spread on the ground | Weak stem that cannot stand upright | Pumpkin, watermelon, sweet potato |

FIG: c6-sci-2-plant-types.svg | Fig. 2.1: Plants grouped by their stems – (a) herb, (b) shrub, (c) tree, (d) climber and (e) creeper

## 2.4 Leaves and Roots: A Secret Link
### Leaf venation
Hold a leaf up against the light. You will see thin lines running across it. These are **veins**. They carry water to all parts of the leaf and food away from it. The pattern formed by the veins is called **venation**.

> **Definition: Venation**
> The arrangement of veins in a leaf is called **venation**.
> - In **reticulate venation**, the veins form a **net-like** pattern on both sides of a thick central vein (the midrib). Examples: mango, rose, hibiscus, peepal.
> - In **parallel venation**, the veins run **side by side**, parallel to one another. Examples: grass, wheat, maize, banana.

> **Activity 2.2: Leaf prints**
> Place a leaf under a sheet of thin paper, with the veined side up. Rub a crayon gently over the paper. The pattern of veins appears! Do this for leaves of grass, mango, banana and tulsi. Label each print as reticulate or parallel.

### Root systems
Gently pull out a small weed and a grass plant from moist soil (after asking your teacher). Wash the soil off the roots and compare them.
- A **taproot** system has one **main, thick root** growing downwards, with smaller **lateral roots** branching from it. Examples: mustard, carrot, radish, mango, rose.
- A **fibrous root** system has **many thin roots** of about the same size, growing from the base of the stem. Examples: wheat, maize, rice, grass, onion.

FIG: c6-sci-2-leaves-roots.svg | Fig. 2.2: (a) Reticulate venation with a taproot and (b) parallel venation with fibrous roots

### The link
Now compare your leaf prints and roots. Do you notice a pattern?

| Leaf venation | Type of root | Examples |
| Reticulate | Taproot | Mustard, rose, hibiscus, mango |
| Parallel | Fibrous roots | Wheat, maize, grass, onion |

> **Remember:** Plants with **reticulate** venation usually have **taproots**. Plants with **parallel** venation usually have **fibrous roots**. So by looking at a leaf, we can often guess what the roots look like without pulling the plant out!

## 2.5 Seeds: Monocots and Dicots
Soak a few gram (chana) seeds and a few maize seeds in water overnight. The next day, gently press a soaked gram seed. It splits into **two** equal halves. Try the same with a maize grain – it does **not** split into two.

Each half of the gram seed is called a **cotyledon** (seed leaf). It stores food for the baby plant.

> **Definition: Monocots and dicots**
> - Plants whose seeds have **two cotyledons** are called **dicotyledons** or **dicots**. Examples: gram, pea, bean, groundnut, mango.
> - Plants whose seeds have only **one cotyledon** are called **monocotyledons** or **monocots**. Examples: maize, wheat, rice, onion.

FIG: c6-sci-2-seeds.svg | Fig. 2.3: (a) A gram seed (dicot) splits into two cotyledons; (b) a maize grain (monocot) has only one

Here is the full picture, putting together what we have learnt:

| Feature | Dicots | Monocots |
| Number of cotyledons in the seed | Two | One |
| Leaf venation | Reticulate | Parallel |
| Root system | Taproot | Fibrous roots |
| Examples | Gram, pea, mustard, mango | Wheat, maize, rice, grass |

> **Example 2.1**
> Riya found a plant whose leaves showed parallel venation. What kind of roots and seeds is it likely to have?
> **Solution:** Plants with parallel venation are usually monocots. So it is likely to have **fibrous roots** and seeds with **one cotyledon**.

## 2.6 Diversity in Animals and their Habitats
Animals are found almost everywhere – in snowy mountains, burning deserts, deep oceans, ponds, forests, grasslands and even inside our homes.

> **Definition: Habitat and adaptation**
> The natural home of a living thing, where it gets **food, water, shelter** and a place to raise its young, is called its **habitat**.
> Special **features** or **habits** that help a living thing survive in its habitat are called **adaptations**.

Habitats on land are called **terrestrial habitats** (forests, deserts, mountains, grasslands). Habitats in water are called **aquatic habitats** (ponds, lakes, rivers, oceans).

| Habitat | Conditions | Animal and its adaptations |
| Desert | Very hot days, cold nights, very little water | **Camel:** long legs keep its body away from hot sand; it can go for many days without water; it passes little urine and its dung is dry; thick eyelashes protect its eyes from sand. |
| Mountains | Very cold, snow, strong winds | **Yak** and **snow leopard:** thick fur keeps them warm. Mountain goats have strong hooves for climbing rocky slopes. |
| Ocean, pond | Water all around | **Fish:** a streamlined body for swimming, **gills** to breathe oxygen dissolved in water, **fins** to move and steer. |
| Grassland | Open land, few places to hide | **Deer:** long legs and strong muscles to run fast; eyes on the sides of the head to watch for danger; sharp ears. |
| Polar regions | Extremely cold, ice everywhere | **Polar bear:** white fur blends with snow; a thick layer of fat keeps it warm. |

FIG: c6-sci-2-habitats.svg | Fig. 2.4: Animals adapted to their habitats – (a) camel in the desert, (b) fish in water and (c) yak in the mountains

Plants are adapted too. Desert plants like the **cactus** have **spines** instead of leaves, and a thick, fleshy stem that stores water. Mountain trees like **pine** and **deodar** are cone-shaped with sloping branches, so that snow slides off easily. The **lotus** in a pond has large, flat, waxy leaves that float on water.

## 2.7 Why Biodiversity Matters
Every living thing has a role in nature:
- **Bees** and **butterflies** carry pollen from flower to flower, helping plants make fruits and seeds.
- **Earthworms** loosen the soil and make it rich.
- **Trees** give us oxygen, food, wood and shade, and are homes for birds and insects.
- **Frogs** and **birds** eat insects and keep their numbers in check.
- **Vultures** clean up the bodies of dead animals.

If even one kind of living thing disappears, others that depend on it are affected. For example, when vulture numbers fell sharply in India, dead animals were left lying around longer, and diseases spread more easily.

> **Did you know?**
> India is one of the world's **mega-diverse** countries. With only about 2.4% of the world's land, it has about **7–8%** of all the recorded kinds of plants and animals!

### How we can protect biodiversity
- Protect forests, wetlands and grasslands – the homes of living things.
- Support **national parks** and **wildlife sanctuaries**, where animals and plants are protected by law.
- Plant native trees, and do not pluck flowers or disturb birds' nests.
- Avoid plastic and do not throw waste into rivers and ponds.

Q: Explain the link between leaf venation and the type of roots. How can it help us?
A: Plants with reticulate (net-like) venation usually have a taproot, like mustard and mango. Plants with parallel venation usually have fibrous roots, like wheat and grass. This helps us guess the root system of a plant just by looking at its leaves, without pulling it out of the soil.
Q: Compare monocots and dicots on the basis of seeds, leaves and roots.
A: Dicots have two cotyledons in their seeds, reticulate venation and taproots (gram, pea, mango). Monocots have one cotyledon, parallel venation and fibrous roots (wheat, maize, rice).
Q: What is biodiversity? Why is it important, and how can we protect it?
A: Biodiversity is the variety of living things in an area. It is important because every living thing has a role – bees pollinate flowers, earthworms enrich soil, trees give oxygen, vultures clean up dead animals – and losing one kind affects others. We can protect it by saving forests and wetlands, supporting national parks and sanctuaries, planting native trees and not polluting.

What did you eat yesterday? Perhaps rice and dal, roti and sabzi, idli and sambar, poha, fish curry, a glass of milk, a banana, or a packet of chips? India is a land of amazing food. What people eat changes from region to region – depending on what grows there, the weather, and family traditions.

Food gives us **energy** to work and play, helps us **grow**, **repairs** our body when we get hurt, and **protects** us from diseases. But not all foods do all these jobs. To stay healthy, we need to know what our food contains and eat it thoughtfully.

In this chapter, we will learn about the **nutrients** in food, how to test foods for some of them, what a **balanced diet** is, what happens when nutrients are missing, and how to eat **mindfully**.

## 3.2 What are Nutrients?
> **Definition: Nutrients**
> The substances in food that our body needs for energy, growth, repair and protection are called **nutrients**.

The main nutrients are **carbohydrates**, **proteins**, **fats**, **vitamins** and **minerals**. Besides these, our body also needs **dietary fibre** and **water**, though they are not counted as nutrients.

Most foods contain more than one nutrient, but usually one of them is present in a larger amount. For example, rice is rich in carbohydrates, while dal is rich in proteins.

## 3.3 Types of Nutrients and their Work
### Carbohydrates – energy-giving foods
Carbohydrates give us **energy**. The main carbohydrates in our food are **starch** and **sugars**.
**Sources:** rice, wheat, maize, millets (ragi, jowar, bajra), potato, sweet potato, sugarcane, jaggery, fruits like banana and mango.

### Fats – stored energy
Fats also give **energy** – in fact, much more energy than the same amount of carbohydrates. Extra fat is stored in the body and used when needed. Fats also keep us warm.
**Sources:** ghee, butter, oil, cream, nuts, groundnuts, cheese, egg yolk.

SAY: buddy-think | Careful, Aarav! Too much fat is stored in the body and can cause health problems. We need fats, but only in small amounts.

### Proteins – body-building foods
Proteins help our body **grow** and **repair** damaged parts. Muscles, skin, hair and nails are all built from proteins. Growing children need plenty of proteins.
**Sources from plants:** pulses (dals), beans, peas, soybean, groundnuts.
**Sources from animals:** milk, paneer, curd, eggs, fish, meat.

### Vitamins – protective foods
Vitamins are needed only in **small amounts**, but they are very important. They **protect** us from diseases and keep our eyes, bones, teeth, gums and skin healthy. Vitamins are named by letters: **A**, **B**, **C**, **D**, **E** and **K**.

| Vitamin | Needed for | Some sources |
| Vitamin A | Healthy eyes and skin | Carrot, papaya, mango, green leafy vegetables, milk |
| Vitamin B (a group) | Using energy from food; healthy nerves | Whole grains, pulses, milk, eggs |
| Vitamin C | Healthy gums; helps fight infections and heal wounds | Amla, orange, lemon, guava, tomato |
| Vitamin D | Strong bones and teeth | Sunlight on our skin, milk, eggs, fish |

> **Did you know?**
> Our skin makes **vitamin D** when it is exposed to sunlight. So playing outdoors in the morning sun is good for your bones!

### Minerals – protective foods
Minerals are also needed in small amounts for good health.

| Mineral | Needed for | Some sources |
| Calcium | Strong bones and teeth | Milk, curd, ragi, green leafy vegetables, sesame |
| Iron | Making blood (haemoglobin) | Green leafy vegetables, jaggery, dates, pulses, meat |
| Iodine | Proper working of the thyroid gland | Iodised salt, seafood |
| Phosphorus | Bones and teeth | Milk, pulses, nuts |

### Dietary fibre and water
**Dietary fibre** (also called **roughage**) does not give nutrients, but it helps food move smoothly through our body and prevents **constipation**. Sources: whole grains, fruits, vegetables, pulses and salad.

**Water** helps the body take in nutrients, removes wastes through urine and sweat, and keeps our body temperature right. We should drink plenty of clean water every day.

## 3.4 Testing Food for Nutrients
How can we find out which nutrients a food contains? Simple tests can help. Do these with your teacher.

> **Activity 3.1: Test for starch**
> **You need:** small pieces of potato, a slice of bread, a little cooked rice, a piece of cucumber, a dropper and **dilute iodine solution**.
> **What to do:** Put each food on a separate plate. Add two or three drops of iodine solution to each.
> **Observation:** Potato, bread and rice turn **blue-black**. Cucumber does not.
> **Conclusion:** A **blue-black** colour shows that the food contains **starch**.

FIG: c6-sci-3-starch-test.svg | Fig. 3.2: Testing for starch – iodine turns a potato slice blue-black

> **Activity 3.2: Test for fat**
> **You need:** a few groundnuts, a little ghee or butter, a piece of boiled potato and some sheets of plain paper.
> **What to do:** Rub each food on a separate piece of paper. Let the paper dry. Hold each paper up against the light.

> **Conclusion:** A **translucent (oily) patch** on paper shows that the food contains **fat**.

> **Activity 3.3: Test for protein**

## 3.5 A Balanced Diet
No single food has all the nutrients in the right amounts. That is why we must eat a **variety** of foods.

> **Definition: Balanced diet**
> A diet that contains **all the nutrients**, along with enough **roughage** and **water**, in the **right amounts** for the needs of our body, is called a **balanced diet**.

A useful way to plan a balanced meal is to think of your plate:
- About **half** the plate: **vegetables and fruits** (vitamins, minerals and fibre).
- About **a quarter**: **cereals and millets** – rice, roti, ragi, jowar (carbohydrates).
- About **a quarter**: **pulses, milk, paneer, eggs, fish or meat** (proteins).
- A little **oil, ghee or nuts** (fats), and a glass of **water**.

FIG: c6-sci-3-plate.svg | Fig. 3.4: A balanced plate – half vegetables and fruits, a quarter cereals and millets, a quarter protein foods

The amount of food we need depends on our **age**, **body size**, **work** and whether we are healthy or ill. A growing child, a farmer working in the fields and an athlete need more energy than a person who sits at a desk all day.

## 3.6 Deficiency Diseases
If a person does not get enough of a particular nutrient for a long time, they may develop a **deficiency disease**.

> **Definition: Deficiency disease**
> A disease caused by the lack of a nutrient in the diet over a long time is called a **deficiency disease**.

| Nutrient lacking | Disease | Symptoms | Foods that help |
| Vitamin A | Night blindness | Poor vision in dim light and at night | Carrot, papaya, mango, green leafy vegetables, milk |
| Vitamin B1 | Beriberi | Weak muscles, very little energy | Whole grains, pulses, unpolished rice |
| Vitamin C | Scurvy | Bleeding gums, wounds take long to heal | Amla, citrus fruits, guava, tomato |
| Vitamin D | Rickets | Soft, bent bones | Sunlight, milk, eggs, fish |
| Calcium | Weak bones and teeth | Bones break easily, tooth decay | Milk, curd, ragi, green leafy vegetables |
| Iodine | Goitre | Swelling of the thyroid gland in the neck | Iodised salt, seafood |
| Iron | Anaemia | Tiredness, pale skin, breathlessness | Green leafy vegetables, jaggery, dates, pulses |

Lack of proteins and energy-giving foods in young children can cause severe weakness and stop them from growing properly. Deficiency diseases can be **prevented** by eating a balanced diet.

## 3.7 Mindful Eating
**Mindful eating** means paying attention to **what**, **how** and **how much** we eat.

## 4.1 Introduction
Have you ever seen a small, colourful object stuck to the door of a refrigerator, holding a list or a photograph in place? Or a pencil box whose lid shuts by itself with a soft "click"? Have you noticed how a few iron pins jump up and cling to certain objects? All of these work because of **magnets**.

SAY: riya-wow | Our fridge has a little mango magnet that never falls off! How does it stick without any glue?
SAY: buddy | Great question, Riya! The fridge door is made of iron or steel, and magnets pull iron towards them. Let's find out more about this amazing power!

There is an interesting old story about how magnets were discovered. It is said that long ago, in a region called Magnesia in ancient Greece, a shepherd named **Magnes** was grazing his sheep on a hillside. His walking stick had an iron tip, and his sandals had iron nails. As he walked over a certain black rock, he felt his stick and sandals being pulled towards the rock and sticking to it! That rock was a natural magnet. Today we call it **magnetite**. Whether the story is exactly true or not, magnetite really is found in nature, and people in many parts of the world, including India, China and Greece, have known about its strange power for thousands of years.

In this chapter, we will explore magnets – what they attract and what they do not, where their strength is greatest, how they help us find directions, how two magnets behave with each other, and how we can make a magnet of our own.

> **Definition: Magnet**
> A **magnet** is an object that attracts (pulls towards itself) objects made of certain materials, such as **iron**, **nickel** and **cobalt**.

### Natural and artificial magnets
Magnets found in nature, like magnetite (also called **lodestone**), are called **natural magnets**. They are usually irregular in shape and not very strong.
Magnets made by people from pieces of iron, steel or special alloys are called **artificial magnets**. They can be made in many useful shapes and are much stronger. Figure 4.1 shows some common shapes.

FIG: c6-sci-4-shapes.svg | Fig. 4.1: Magnets of different shapes – (a) bar, (b) horseshoe (U-shaped), (c) ring, (d) cylindrical and (e) needle magnet

## 4.2 Magnetic and Non-magnetic Materials
Does a magnet attract everything? Let us find out with a simple activity.

> **Activity 4.1: Which objects does a magnet attract?**
> **You need:** a bar magnet and a collection of objects – an iron nail, a steel spoon, a coin, a pencil, an eraser, a plastic scale, a paper clip, a piece of cloth, a key, a rubber band, an aluminium foil and a glass marble.
> **What to do:** Bring the magnet close to each object one by one. Note whether the object is pulled towards the magnet and sticks to it.

| Iron nail | Iron | Yes |
| Paper clip | Iron/steel | Yes |
| Steel spoon | Steel (mostly iron) | Yes (most steel spoons) |
| Key | Iron or brass | Depends on the metal |

You will find that the magnet attracts only some of the objects. > **Definition: Magnetic and non-magnetic materials**
> Materials that are attracted by a magnet are called **magnetic materials** – for example, **iron, nickel, cobalt** and steel.
> Materials that are not attracted by a magnet are called **non-magnetic materials** – for example, wood, plastic, rubber, paper, glass, cloth, aluminium and copper.

> **Remember:** "Metal" does not always mean "magnetic". A magnet attracts iron, nickel and cobalt, but not most other metals like aluminium, copper or gold.

> **Example 4.1**

## 4.3 Poles of a Magnet
Is the attraction of a magnet equally strong everywhere on it? Let us investigate.

> **Activity 4.2: Where is a magnet strongest?**
> Spread some **iron filings** (tiny bits of iron) on a sheet of paper. Place a bar magnet on them and gently roll it over. Lift the magnet and look at it carefully.
> **Observation:** Most of the iron filings stick near the two **ends** of the magnet. Very few stick to the middle.

FIG: c6-sci-4-filings.svg | Fig. 4.2: Iron filings cling mostly near the ends of a bar magnet

The two ends of a magnet, where its attraction is strongest, are called its **poles**. Every magnet has **two poles** – a **North pole (N)** and a **South pole (S)**. This is true for all magnets, whatever their shape: a horseshoe magnet has its poles at the two ends of the "U", a ring magnet has them on its two flat faces, and a cylindrical magnet has them at the two round ends.

> **Did you know?**
> If you break a bar magnet into two pieces, you do not get a separate North pole and South pole. Each piece becomes a complete magnet with **both** a North and a South pole! However many times you break it, every piece has two poles.

SAY: aarav-wow | So if I break a magnet into ten pieces, I get ten tiny magnets?
SAY: buddy | Exactly! Each little piece will have its own North pole and South pole.

## 4.4 Finding Directions using a Magnet
People have used magnets to find their way for centuries. How? Let us find out.

> **Activity 4.3: A freely suspended magnet**
> Tie a thread around the middle of a bar magnet so that it balances. Hang it from a wooden stand (not an iron one) where there is no other magnet or iron object nearby. Let it come to rest. Mark the direction of its ends on the floor.
> Now turn the magnet gently and let it settle again. Repeat this a few times.
> **Observation:** Every time, the magnet comes to rest in the **same direction** – roughly **north–south**.

A freely suspended magnet always comes to rest in the **north–south direction**. The end of the magnet that points towards the **geographic north** is called its **north-seeking pole** or **North pole**; the other end, pointing south, is its **South pole**. This is how the poles of a magnet got their names.

### The magnetic compass
A **compass** uses this property. It is a small box with a glass cover, containing a light **magnetised needle** that can turn freely on a pin. The North pole of the needle is usually painted red. A circular dial shows the directions – North, South, East and West.

FIG: c6-sci-4-compass.svg | Fig. 4.3: A magnetic compass – the red end of the needle points towards north

To use a compass, place it on a flat surface away from iron objects and wait for the needle to stop. Then turn the box gently until the "N" on the dial lines up with the red end of the needle. All the other directions can now be read from the dial.

SAY: riya | On our school trek, the guide used a compass to lead us back to the camp!
SAY: buddy | That's how hikers, sailors and pilots find their way. The needle always settles in the north–south direction.

> **Did you know?**
> Long before modern instruments, sailors and travellers used **lodestones** and magnetised needles to find directions at sea, especially on cloudy nights when the stars could not be seen. In ancient China, a spoon carved from lodestone was placed on a smooth bronze plate – it always turned to point **south**! This was one of the earliest compasses.

## 4.5 Attraction and Repulsion between Magnets
What happens when the poles of two magnets are brought close to each other?

> **Activity 4.4: Two magnets meet**
> Take two bar magnets with their poles marked. Place one on a table. Bring the North pole of the second magnet close to the North pole of the first one. Then try North near South, and South near South.
> **Observation:** When the **same poles** face each other (N–N or S–S), the magnets **push away** from each other. When **different poles** face each other (N–S), they **pull together** and stick.

FIG: c6-sci-4-attract-repel.svg | Fig. 4.4: Unlike poles attract each other; like poles repel each other

From this activity we learn a basic law of magnets:
> **Remember:** **Like poles repel; unlike poles attract.**
> N and N repel. S and S repel. N and S attract.

### The sure test for a magnet
Suppose you have two identical-looking iron bars, and only one is a magnet. How can you tell which one? If the bar **attracts** a pole of your magnet, it could be either a magnet (unlike pole) or just a piece of iron (iron is attracted by both poles). But if it **repels** one pole of your magnet, it **must** be a magnet – only a magnet can repel a magnet.
> **Remember:** **Repulsion is the sure test of magnetism.**

SAY: aarav-think | I tried to push two North poles together, but they kept sliding away – as if they don't want to be friends!
SAY: buddy-wow | Ha ha! That's repulsion – like poles repel. Turn one magnet around and – click! – unlike poles attract.

> **Example 4.2**
> A boy brings the North pole of a magnet near one end of an iron bar, and the bar is attracted. Can he conclude that the bar is a magnet?
> **Solution:** No. A plain iron bar is also attracted by a magnet. He should bring the North pole near the same end again and check the other end too. If any end is **repelled**, the bar is a magnet.

## 4.6 Making Your Own Magnet
You can turn an ordinary piece of iron into a magnet yourself!

> **Activity 4.5: Make a magnet by stroking**
> **You need:** a bar magnet and a long iron nail (or an iron strip or a large sewing needle).
> **What to do:** Place the nail on a table. Put one pole of the bar magnet near the **head** of the nail and slide it along to the **tip**. Then lift the magnet high above the nail and bring it back to the head. Repeat this **30–40 times**, always moving the magnet in the **same direction** and using the **same pole**.
> **Test it:** Bring the nail near some iron pins. Do they stick to it? Your nail has become a magnet!

FIG: c6-sci-4-magnetise.svg | Fig. 4.5: Making a magnet by stroking an iron nail in one direction

Magnets made in this way are **temporary** – they lose their magnetism after some time. Factories make stronger, longer-lasting magnets using electricity.

> **Activity 4.6: Make your own compass**
> Magnetise a sewing needle using Activity 4.5. Push it through a small piece of cork (or stick it on a flat piece of thermocol) and float it in a bowl of water. Keep the bowl away from iron objects.
> **Observation:** The needle turns and comes to rest in the north–south direction. You have made a working compass!

## 4.7 Uses of Magnets
Magnets are used all around us:
- **At home:** refrigerator doors and fridge magnets, magnetic pencil boxes and bag clasps, cupboard latches.
- **Electronics:** speakers, earphones, microphones, electric motors in fans and mixers, and generators that produce electricity.
- **Finding directions:** compasses used by sailors, pilots, soldiers and trekkers.
- **Industry:** large **electromagnets** fitted to cranes lift heavy scrap iron; magnets separate iron pieces from waste.
- **Medicine:** **MRI** machines in hospitals use powerful magnets to take pictures of the inside of the body.
- **Transport:** some fast trains, called **maglev** (magnetic levitation) trains, float above the track using magnets, so there is no rubbing and they can go very fast.
- **Toys and games:** magnetic building blocks, puzzles and fishing games.

## 4.8 Taking Care of Magnets
Magnets can **lose their magnetism** if they are:
- **heated** strongly,
- **hammered** or **dropped** from a height,
- kept carelessly, loosely in a box.

To keep bar magnets strong, store them **in pairs** with **unlike poles side by side**, separated by a piece of wood, with two pieces of soft iron, called **keepers**, placed across their ends (Fig. 4.6). Horseshoe magnets should be kept with a piece of iron across their poles.

> **Remember:** Keep magnets away from mobile phones, computers, televisions, cassettes and bank cards, as magnets can damage them.

## Summary
- A **magnet** attracts objects made of **magnetic materials** such as iron, nickel and cobalt.
- Materials not attracted by magnets are **non-magnetic** – wood, plastic, glass, rubber, aluminium and copper.
- **Natural magnets** (magnetite or lodestone) occur in nature; **artificial magnets** are made in shapes such as bar, horseshoe, ring, cylinder and needle.
- The attraction of a magnet is strongest at its **poles**. Every magnet has two poles – **North** and **South**.
- A freely suspended magnet always rests in the **north–south** direction. A **compass** uses this property to find directions.
- **Like poles repel; unlike poles attract.** **Repulsion** is the sure test of a magnet.
- A magnet can be made by **stroking** an iron object with a magnet in one direction.
- Magnets lose their magnetism when heated, hammered or dropped; they should be stored with **keepers**.

## 🤔 What is measurement?
> 📌 **Measurement** is comparing an unknown quantity with a known fixed quantity called a **unit**. Every measurement has a **number** and a **unit**: 3 **metres**.
**Standard units** are the same everywhere. Scientists worldwide use the **SI system** (International System of Units).
- The SI unit of length is the **metre (m)**.

## 📏 Measuring correctly with a ruler
1. Place the ruler **along** the object, touching it.
2. Start at **0** (or at a clear mark like 1 cm if the edge is broken, and subtract it).
3. Keep your eye **directly above** the reading – looking from the side gives a wrong reading.
4. To measure a **curved line**, lay a thread along it, mark the ends, and measure the straightened thread.

## 🚗 What is motion?
> 📌 An object is in **motion** if its **position changes with time**. If it doesn't change, it is **at rest**.
Motion is described with respect to something else (a **reference point**). A passenger is at rest compared to the bus seat but in motion compared to a tree outside!

SAY: riya-wow | A merry-go-round shows circular motion!

## 🧩 Types of motion
- **Linear (rectilinear) motion:** along a straight line – a train on a straight track, a falling apple, a march-past.
- **Circular motion:** along a circular path – a merry-go-round, the tip of a fan blade, a stone whirled on a string.
- **Oscillatory (periodic) motion:** to-and-fro about a fixed point, repeating – a swing, a pendulum, a plucked sitar string.
> 💡 Many objects show more than one motion. A bicycle wheel spins (circular) while the bicycle moves forward (linear).

## 🌍 In real life
Tailors use measuring tapes, doctors measure height, athletes time their races, and GPS measures distances on maps.

## 🤔 What is temperature?
> 📌 **Temperature** tells us **how hot or cold** something is. It is measured with a **thermometer**. The common unit is the **degree Celsius (°C)**.
- Water freezes at **0 °C** and boils at **100 °C** (at normal pressure).

## 🧩 Types of thermometers
**1. Clinical thermometer** – measures **body temperature**.
- Range: about **35 °C to 42 °C** (our body never goes far outside this).
- Average normal body temperature is about **37 °C** (it can vary a little from person to person and during the day).
- It has a **kink** (a bend) that stops the mercury level from falling back, so you can read it after taking it out.
- Shake it gently before use to bring the level below 35 °C.

**2. Laboratory thermometer** – measures temperatures of other things (water, liquids).
- Range: about **–10 °C to 110 °C**.
- No kink – read it **while it is still in** the liquid.
- Hold it upright; the bulb should not touch the container.

**3. Digital thermometer** – shows the number on a screen. Safe and easy, now widely used. Infrared thermometers measure without touching.

## ⚠️ Mercury safety
Older thermometers contain **mercury**, which is poisonous. If one breaks, don't touch it – tell an adult. Many countries are replacing them with digital ones.

## ⚙️ Reading a thermometer correctly
1. Note the range and what each small division stands for.
2. Keep your eye **level** with the top of the liquid.
3. For a clinical thermometer, keep the bulb under the tongue or in the armpit for about 1 minute.

## 🌍 In real life
Weather reports, fridges and ovens, fever checks, and keeping vaccines and medicines at the right temperature.

## 🤔 The three states of water
> 📌 Matter exists in three main **states**: **solid**, **liquid** and **gas**. Water can be in all three!
- **Solid – ice:** has a fixed shape and fixed volume.
- **Liquid – water:** has a fixed volume but takes the shape of its container.
- **Gas – water vapour:** has no fixed shape or volume; spreads out to fill any space. It is invisible!
> 💡 The white "smoke" from a kettle is not vapour – it's tiny water droplets formed when vapour cools. Real vapour is invisible.

## 🔄 Changes of state
- **Melting:** solid → liquid (ice melts at **0 °C**).
- **Freezing:** liquid → solid (water freezes at **0 °C**).
- **Evaporation:** liquid → gas, slowly at any temperature, from the surface.
- **Boiling:** liquid → gas, quickly, throughout the liquid (water boils at **100 °C**).
- **Condensation:** gas → liquid on cooling (drops outside a cold glass, dew on grass).

## ⚙️ What makes evaporation faster?
1. **Higher temperature** – clothes dry faster on a sunny day.
2. **More surface area** – spread clothes out; water in a plate dries faster than in a bottle.
3. **Wind** – clothes dry faster on a windy day.
4. **Less humidity** (less moisture in the air) – clothes dry slowly in the rainy season.

SAY: aarav-think | That's why sweating cools us – evaporation causes cooling!

## ❄️ Evaporation causes cooling
When water evaporates, it takes heat from the surface. That's why:
- We feel cool when sweat dries.
- Water in an earthen **matka** stays cool – water seeps through tiny pores and evaporates.
- Sprinkling water on a hot roof cools it.

## 🌧️ The water cycle
1. The sun heats water in seas, rivers and lakes → **evaporation**. Plants also give out water vapour (**transpiration**).
2. Vapour rises, cools and **condenses** into tiny droplets → **clouds**.
3. Droplets join and become heavy → **precipitation** (rain, snow, hail).
4. Water flows into rivers and seas or soaks into the ground (**groundwater**) → and the cycle repeats!

## ✋ Try it yourself

## ⚠️ Common mistakes
- Thinking evaporation happens only at 100 °C. It happens at all temperatures; boiling happens at 100 °C.
- Calling the visible steam "water vapour".

## 🧠 Quick recap
- Water: solid (ice), liquid (water), gas (vapour).
- Melting, freezing, evaporation, boiling, condensation.
- Evaporation is faster with heat, wind, more surface area, low humidity; it cools.
- The water cycle keeps water moving.

## 📖 New words
- **Evaporation** – liquid slowly turning into gas
- **Condensation** – gas turning into liquid on cooling
- **Humidity** – amount of water vapour in the air

- **Magnetic separation:** using a magnet to pull out **iron** pieces from a mixture.

## 🤔 What are natural resources?
> 📌 **Natural resources** are materials and energy we get from **nature** that are useful to us – air, water, sunlight, soil, plants, animals, minerals, coal and petroleum.

## 🧩 Types of natural resources
**1. Renewable resources** – are replaced naturally in a short time, if used wisely.
- **Air, water, sunlight, wind, forests.**
**2. Non-renewable resources** – are limited; they take **millions of years** to form and will run out.
- **Coal, petroleum, natural gas, minerals** (iron, copper).
> 💡 Coal and petroleum are called **fossil fuels** because they formed from remains of plants and animals buried long ago.

## 🌬️ Air
A mixture of gases – mainly **nitrogen (about 78%)** and **oxygen (about 21%)**, plus carbon dioxide and others. We need oxygen to breathe; plants need carbon dioxide to make food.

## 💧 Water
Needed by all living things. Only a tiny part of Earth's water is fresh and usable. Save it!

## ☀️ Sunlight and wind – clean energy
- **Solar energy:** solar panels turn sunlight into electricity; solar cookers cook food.
- **Wind energy:** windmills and wind turbines turn moving air into electricity. Tamil Nadu and Gujarat have many wind farms.
These don't pollute and won't run out!

## 🪨 Soil and forests
Soil grows our food and takes hundreds of years to form just a few centimetres. Forests give oxygen, wood, fruits, medicines and homes to animals, and bring rain.

## ♻️ Using resources wisely
- **Reduce, Reuse, Recycle.**
- Switch off lights and fans when not needed.
- Walk, cycle or use public transport.
- Plant trees; don't waste paper, food or water.
> 😮 Gandhiji said: "The Earth provides enough to satisfy every man's need, but not every man's greed."

## ✋ Try it yourself
Do a "resource audit" at home for one day: count how many times a tap, a light or a fan was left on unnecessarily.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes
- Thinking water is non-renewable – it is renewable through the water cycle, but clean fresh water is limited and must be saved.
- Thinking forests can be cut without limit because they are "renewable". They regrow only if we replant and protect them.

## 🧠 Quick recap
- Natural resources come from nature.
- Renewable: air, water, sunlight, wind, forests. Non-renewable: coal, petroleum, natural gas, minerals.
- Solar and wind energy are clean.
- Use resources wisely: reduce, reuse, recycle.

## 📖 New words
- **Renewable** – can be replaced naturally
- **Fossil fuel** – fuel formed from ancient remains (coal, petroleum)
- **Conservation** – careful use and protection

## 🤔 Stars
> 📌 A **star** is a huge, very hot ball of glowing gases that gives out its **own light and heat**.
- The **Sun** is our nearest star. Other stars look tiny because they are very, very far away.
- Stars seem to **twinkle** because their light passes through moving layers of air.
- Stars appear to move across the night sky from east to west because the **Earth rotates**.

## ✨ Constellations
> 📌 A **constellation** is a group of stars that forms a recognisable pattern in the sky.
- **Saptarishi (Ursa Major / Big Dipper):** seven bright stars shaped like a ladle or a question mark.
- **Orion (Hunter / Mriga):** three bright stars in a row make the hunter's **belt**.
- **Cassiopeia:** looks like a W or M.
- The **Pole Star (Dhruva Tara)** stays almost fixed above the north. The pointer stars of Saptarishi point to it – it helps us find **north**!

## 🪐 Our solar system
> 📌 The **solar system** is the Sun and all the objects that move around it.
- **Eight planets**, in order from the Sun: **Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune.**
- Trick: "**M**y **V**ery **E**ager **M**other **J**ust **S**erved **U**s **N**oodles."
- **Planets** don't make their own light; they reflect sunlight and don't twinkle.
- Inner planets (Mercury–Mars) are **rocky**; outer planets (Jupiter–Neptune) are **giant** and mostly gas or ice.
- **Venus** is the brightest planet in our sky; **Jupiter** is the largest; **Saturn** has beautiful rings; **Mars** is red.
- **Pluto** is now called a **dwarf planet**.

## 🌙 Other members
- **Moons (satellites):** objects that go around planets. Earth has **one** Moon; it reflects sunlight.
- **Asteroids:** rocky pieces, mostly in the **asteroid belt** between Mars and Jupiter.
- **Comets:** balls of ice and dust with glowing **tails** when near the Sun – Halley's Comet returns about every 76 years.
- **Meteors:** small rocks burning up in our atmosphere – "shooting stars".

## 🇮🇳 India in space
- **Aryabhata** (1975) – India's first satellite, named after the great astronomer.
- **Chandrayaan-3** (2023) – landed near the Moon's south pole; India celebrates **23 August as National Space Day**.
- **Aditya-L1** – India's mission to study the Sun.

## 🌃 Light pollution
Too many bright city lights hide the stars. Using lights wisely helps us – and birds and insects – see the night sky.

## ✋ Try it yourself
On a clear night, find Saptarishi. Follow the two "pointer" stars to locate the Pole Star.

## ⚠️ Common mistakes
- Calling the Moon a star or a planet. It's Earth's natural satellite.
- Thinking a "shooting star" is a falling star – it's a small rock burning in our atmosphere.

## 🧠 Quick recap
- Stars make their own light; planets reflect it.
- Constellations are star patterns; the Pole Star shows north.
- Eight planets go around the Sun; Earth has one Moon.
- India's space missions: Aryabhata, Chandrayaan, Aditya-L1.

## 📖 New words
- **Constellation** – a pattern of stars
- **Satellite** – an object that goes around a planet
- **Orbit** – the path of an object around another
