## 1.1 Introduction
Look around you. Why does a ball thrown up always come back down? Why does milk turn into curd overnight? Why does the Moon look like a thin silver line on some nights and a full round plate on others? Why do the leaves of a touch-me-not plant fold up when you touch them?

You ask questions like these every day. Each time you wonder "**why?**", "**how?**" or "**what if?**", you are thinking like a scientist. Science begins with **curiosity** – the wish to know and understand.

SAY: riya-think | Is science only done by people in white coats, inside big laboratories?
SAY: buddy | Not at all, Riya! Science happens in kitchens, gardens, playgrounds and fields – anywhere someone asks a question and looks carefully for the answer.

Science is not just a collection of facts written in books. It is a **way of finding out** about the world – by looking closely, asking questions, making careful guesses, testing them, and accepting only what the evidence shows. Anybody can do it, and everybody uses its results: the light bulb in your room, the medicine that cures a fever, the phone in your family's hands and the bus that brings you to school all exist because of science.

> **Definition: Science**
> **Science** is a way of learning about the natural world by **observing**, **asking questions**, **testing ideas** through experiments, and drawing conclusions based on **evidence**.

In this chapter, you will learn how scientists work, what the main branches of science are, which tools help us observe, and how scientists – including many from India – have changed the way we live.

## 1.2 Science Begins with Observation
To **observe** means to notice things carefully using our **senses** – sight, hearing, touch, smell and taste. A scientist observes not just once, but again and again, and writes down what is seen.

> **Activity 1.1: Be a careful observer**
> **You need:** a leaf, a stone, a hand lens (magnifying glass) and your notebook.
> **What to do:** Look at the leaf with your eyes first and write down three things you notice. Then look at it through the hand lens. Do the same with the stone.
> **Think:** Did you notice new things through the lens – tiny veins, hairs or shiny grains? How many more observations could you write?

Good observations are **careful**, **honest** and **recorded**. Writing them down helps us compare and remember. Sometimes we need **tools** to observe things our senses cannot catch – things that are too small, too far, too hot or too cold.

### Tools that help us observe
| Tool | What it helps us do |
| Hand lens (magnifying glass) | See small things in more detail |
| Microscope | See very tiny things, such as cells, that we cannot see with our eyes alone |
| Telescope | See faraway objects like the Moon, planets and stars |
| Thermometer | Measure how hot or cold something is |
| Measuring scale and tape | Measure length |
| Weighing balance | Measure mass |
| Measuring cylinder | Measure the volume of a liquid |
| Stopwatch | Measure time |

> **Did you know?**
> Our senses can sometimes be fooled! Put one hand in cold water and the other in warm water for a minute. Then dip both hands in the same bowl of normal water. One hand feels it is warm and the other feels it is cold! That is why scientists use measuring tools such as thermometers instead of relying only on feelings.

## 1.3 How Scientists Work: The Scientific Method
Scientists follow a set of steps to find reliable answers. These steps are together called the **scientific method**. Let us follow Aarav as he uses it.

SAY: aarav-think | The money plant on our window always bends towards the glass. Is it trying to look outside?
SAY: buddy | That's a perfect observation to start with, Aarav! Let's use the scientific method to find out why.

**Step 1 – Observe:** Aarav notices that the plant near the window grows bent towards the window.
**Step 2 – Ask a question:** Why does the plant bend towards the window?
**Step 3 – Make a hypothesis:** A **hypothesis** is a sensible guess that can be tested. Aarav guesses: "Plants grow towards light."
**Step 4 – Test it with an experiment:** He takes two similar potted plants. He places one inside a cardboard box with a hole cut in one side. The other is kept in the open, with light all around it. Both get the same soil, the same water and the same pot.
**Step 5 – Observe and record the results:** After a week, the plant in the box has bent towards the hole. The plant in the open has grown straight up.
**Step 6 – Conclude:** The evidence supports his hypothesis – plants grow towards light.
**Step 7 – Share and ask more questions:** Aarav tells his class. Riya asks: "Will it happen with every plant? With coloured light?" New questions start the method all over again!

### A fair test
In Aarav's experiment, only **one** thing was different between the two plants – the light. Everything else was kept the same. This is called a **fair test**.

> **Definition: Fair test and variable**
> Anything that can change in an experiment is called a **variable**. In a **fair test**, we change **only one variable** at a time and keep all the others the same, so that we know exactly what caused the result.

> **Example 1.1**
> Riya wants to find out whether seeds sprout faster in warm or in cold places. She keeps 10 moong seeds on wet cotton near the warm kitchen window and 10 bean seeds on dry cotton in the cold storeroom. Is this a fair test?
> **Solution:** No. She has changed three things – the place (warm/cold), the kind of seed (moong/bean) and the water (wet/dry). She should use the **same kind of seed** and the **same wet cotton** in both, and change **only the temperature**.

SAY: riya-wow | So if I change too many things together, I can never know which one made the difference!

### Why scientists repeat experiments
A single result can happen by chance. Scientists **repeat** their experiments many times, and other scientists repeat them too. If everyone gets the same result, we can trust it. If new evidence shows an idea is wrong, scientists change their idea. This honesty is what makes science powerful.

> **Remember:** In science, we believe an idea because of **evidence**, not because many people say it or because it is written somewhere.

## 1.4 Branches of Science
The world is so vast that science is divided into different **branches**, each studying one part of it.

| Branch | What it studies | Example of a question it asks |
| Biology | Living things – plants, animals, the human body | How does food give us energy? |
| Physics | Matter, motion, force, energy, light, sound, electricity | Why does a ball roll down a slope? |
| Chemistry | Materials, what they are made of and how they change | Why does iron rust? |
| Earth science | Rocks, soil, water, weather and the Earth itself | What causes earthquakes? |
| Astronomy | Stars, planets, the Moon and outer space | Why does the Moon change shape? |

FIG: c6-sci-1-branches.svg | Fig. 1.4: The main branches of science and some things they study

The branches are connected. For example, a doctor uses biology (the body), chemistry (medicines) and physics (X-ray machines) to treat a patient. Many of today's most exciting discoveries happen where two branches meet.

## 1.5 Science All Around Us
Science is part of everything we do, even when we do not notice it.
- **In the kitchen:** cooking changes raw food; sugar dissolves in tea; a pressure cooker cooks food faster by trapping steam.
- **On the playground:** a swing moves back and forth; a ball bounces; a bicycle stays balanced while moving.
- **In nature:** a rainbow forms when sunlight splits into colours; seeds sprout after rain; birds fly south in winter.
- **In our health:** vaccines protect us from diseases; clean water and soap keep germs away.
- **In technology:** mobile phones, computers, electricity and satellites that bring us weather reports and maps.

### Science and our responsibility
Science gives us great power, and we must use it wisely. Plastics made life easier, but they also pollute rivers and oceans. Fuels run our vehicles, but their smoke dirties the air. A good scientist – and a good citizen – thinks about how discoveries affect people, animals and the planet.

- The **scientific method**: observe → ask a question → make a hypothesis → experiment → record results → conclude → share and ask new questions.
- A **hypothesis** is a sensible guess that can be tested.
- In a **fair test**, only **one variable** is changed at a time.
- Experiments are **repeated** to make sure results are reliable.

Take a slow walk around your school or your neighbourhood. Living things differ from one another in size, shape, colour, the places where they live and the food they eat. Yet they are also similar in many ways. Studying these **similarities and differences** helps us group living things and understand them better.

> **Definition: Diversity and biodiversity**
> **Diversity** means **variety**. **Biodiversity** is the variety of all the different kinds of plants, animals and other living things found in an area.

In this chapter, we will learn how plants can be grouped by their stems, leaves, roots and seeds, how animals are suited to the places where they live, and why biodiversity is precious.

## 2.2 Grouping Living Things
Imagine a library where the books are placed on shelves at random. Finding one book would take hours! Libraries **group** books – stories on one shelf, science on another. In the same way, scientists group living things based on their features. Grouping makes it easier to study, compare and remember them.

> **Activity 2.1: Sort and group**
> **You need:** pictures or real samples of 12 plants and animals (for example, mango, grass, rose, money plant, pumpkin, sparrow, fish, cow, butterfly, frog, earthworm, snake).
> **What to do:** Sort them into groups. You can choose any feature – where they live, how they move, what they eat, whether they have legs.
> **Think:** Did your friends make the same groups? Is there more than one correct way to group living things?

You will find that there are many ways to group. Scientists choose features that are clear and easy to check.

## 2.3 Grouping Plants by their Stems
Look at the **stem** of a plant. Is it soft and green, or hard and woody? Is it thin and weak, or thick and strong? Based on their stems and height, plants are grouped as herbs, shrubs and trees. Plants with weak stems are called climbers or creepers.

| Group | Height | Stem | Examples |
| Herbs | Short, usually less than 1 metre | Soft, green, tender | Tulsi, mint, coriander, wheat, grass |
| Shrubs | Medium height | Hard and woody; many branches start near the base | Rose, hibiscus, lemon, jasmine |
| Trees | Tall | Thick, hard, woody **trunk**; branches start high above the ground | Mango, neem, banyan, peepal |
| Climbers | Grow upwards with support | Weak stem that climbs using tendrils or by twining | Money plant, grapevine, pea, bitter gourd |
| Creepers | Spread on the ground | Weak stem that cannot stand upright | Pumpkin, watermelon, sweet potato |

FIG: c6-sci-2-plant-types.svg | Fig. 2.1: Plants grouped by their stems – (a) herb, (b) shrub, (c) tree, (d) climber and (e) creeper

## 2.4 Leaves and Roots: A Secret Link
### Leaf venation
Hold a leaf up against the light. You will see thin lines running across it. These are **veins**. They carry water to all parts of the leaf and food away from it. The pattern formed by the veins is called **venation**.

> **Definition: Venation**
> The arrangement of veins in a leaf is called **venation**.
> - In **reticulate venation**, the veins form a **net-like** pattern on both sides of a thick central vein (the midrib). Examples: mango, rose, hibiscus, peepal.
> - In **parallel venation**, the veins run **side by side**, parallel to one another. Examples: grass, wheat, maize, banana.

> **Activity 2.2: Leaf prints**
> Place a leaf under a sheet of thin paper, with the veined side up. Rub a crayon gently over the paper. The pattern of veins appears! Do this for leaves of grass, mango, banana and tulsi. Label each print as reticulate or parallel.

### Root systems
Gently pull out a small weed and a grass plant from moist soil (after asking your teacher). Wash the soil off the roots and compare them.
- A **taproot** system has one **main, thick root** growing downwards, with smaller **lateral roots** branching from it. Examples: mustard, carrot, radish, mango, rose.
- A **fibrous root** system has **many thin roots** of about the same size, growing from the base of the stem. Examples: wheat, maize, rice, grass, onion.

FIG: c6-sci-2-leaves-roots.svg | Fig. 2.2: (a) Reticulate venation with a taproot and (b) parallel venation with fibrous roots

### The link
Now compare your leaf prints and roots. Do you notice a pattern?

| Leaf venation | Type of root | Examples |
| Reticulate | Taproot | Mustard, rose, hibiscus, mango |
| Parallel | Fibrous roots | Wheat, maize, grass, onion |

> **Remember:** Plants with **reticulate** venation usually have **taproots**. Plants with **parallel** venation usually have **fibrous roots**. So by looking at a leaf, we can often guess what the roots look like without pulling the plant out!

## 2.5 Seeds: Monocots and Dicots
Soak a few gram (chana) seeds and a few maize seeds in water overnight. The next day, gently press a soaked gram seed. It splits into **two** equal halves. Try the same with a maize grain – it does **not** split into two.

Each half of the gram seed is called a **cotyledon** (seed leaf). It stores food for the baby plant.

> **Definition: Monocots and dicots**
> - Plants whose seeds have **two cotyledons** are called **dicotyledons** or **dicots**. Examples: gram, pea, bean, groundnut, mango.
> - Plants whose seeds have only **one cotyledon** are called **monocotyledons** or **monocots**. Examples: maize, wheat, rice, onion.

FIG: c6-sci-2-seeds.svg | Fig. 2.3: (a) A gram seed (dicot) splits into two cotyledons; (b) a maize grain (monocot) has only one

Here is the full picture, putting together what we have learnt:

| Feature | Dicots | Monocots |
| Number of cotyledons in the seed | Two | One |
| Leaf venation | Reticulate | Parallel |
| Root system | Taproot | Fibrous roots |
| Examples | Gram, pea, mustard, mango | Wheat, maize, rice, grass |

> **Example 2.1**
> Riya found a plant whose leaves showed parallel venation. What kind of roots and seeds is it likely to have?
> **Solution:** Plants with parallel venation are usually monocots. So it is likely to have **fibrous roots** and seeds with **one cotyledon**.

## 2.6 Diversity in Animals and their Habitats
Animals are found almost everywhere – in snowy mountains, burning deserts, deep oceans, ponds, forests, grasslands and even inside our homes.

> **Definition: Habitat and adaptation**
> The natural home of a living thing, where it gets **food, water, shelter** and a place to raise its young, is called its **habitat**.
> Special **features** or **habits** that help a living thing survive in its habitat are called **adaptations**.

Habitats on land are called **terrestrial habitats** (forests, deserts, mountains, grasslands). Habitats in water are called **aquatic habitats** (ponds, lakes, rivers, oceans).

| Habitat | Conditions | Animal and its adaptations |
| Desert | Very hot days, cold nights, very little water | **Camel:** long legs keep its body away from hot sand; it can go for many days without water; it passes little urine and its dung is dry; thick eyelashes protect its eyes from sand. |
| Mountains | Very cold, snow, strong winds | **Yak** and **snow leopard:** thick fur keeps them warm. Mountain goats have strong hooves for climbing rocky slopes. |
| Ocean, pond | Water all around | **Fish:** a streamlined body for swimming, **gills** to breathe oxygen dissolved in water, **fins** to move and steer. |
| Grassland | Open land, few places to hide | **Deer:** long legs and strong muscles to run fast; eyes on the sides of the head to watch for danger; sharp ears. |
| Polar regions | Extremely cold, ice everywhere | **Polar bear:** white fur blends with snow; a thick layer of fat keeps it warm. |

FIG: c6-sci-2-habitats.svg | Fig. 2.4: Animals adapted to their habitats – (a) camel in the desert, (b) fish in water and (c) yak in the mountains

Plants are adapted too. Desert plants like the **cactus** have **spines** instead of leaves, and a thick, fleshy stem that stores water. Mountain trees like **pine** and **deodar** are cone-shaped with sloping branches, so that snow slides off easily. The **lotus** in a pond has large, flat, waxy leaves that float on water.

## 2.7 Why Biodiversity Matters
Every living thing has a role in nature:
- **Bees** and **butterflies** carry pollen from flower to flower, helping plants make fruits and seeds.
- **Earthworms** loosen the soil and make it rich.
- **Trees** give us oxygen, food, wood and shade, and are homes for birds and insects.
- **Frogs** and **birds** eat insects and keep their numbers in check.
- **Vultures** clean up the bodies of dead animals.

If even one kind of living thing disappears, others that depend on it are affected. For example, when vulture numbers fell sharply in India, dead animals were left lying around longer, and diseases spread more easily.

> **Did you know?**
> India is one of the world's **mega-diverse** countries. With only about 2.4% of the world's land, it has about **7–8%** of all the recorded kinds of plants and animals!

### How we can protect biodiversity
- Protect forests, wetlands and grasslands – the homes of living things.
- Support **national parks** and **wildlife sanctuaries**, where animals and plants are protected by law.
- Plant native trees, and do not pluck flowers or disturb birds' nests.
- Avoid plastic and do not throw waste into rivers and ponds.

Q: Explain the link between leaf venation and the type of roots. How can it help us?
A: Plants with reticulate (net-like) venation usually have a taproot, like mustard and mango. Plants with parallel venation usually have fibrous roots, like wheat and grass. This helps us guess the root system of a plant just by looking at its leaves, without pulling it out of the soil.
Q: Compare monocots and dicots on the basis of seeds, leaves and roots.
A: Dicots have two cotyledons in their seeds, reticulate venation and taproots (gram, pea, mango). Monocots have one cotyledon, parallel venation and fibrous roots (wheat, maize, rice).
Q: What is biodiversity? Why is it important, and how can we protect it?
A: Biodiversity is the variety of living things in an area. It is important because every living thing has a role – bees pollinate flowers, earthworms enrich soil, trees give oxygen, vultures clean up dead animals – and losing one kind affects others. We can protect it by saving forests and wetlands, supporting national parks and sanctuaries, planting native trees and not polluting.

What did you eat yesterday? Perhaps rice and dal, roti and sabzi, idli and sambar, poha, fish curry, a glass of milk, a banana, or a packet of chips? India is a land of amazing food. What people eat changes from region to region – depending on what grows there, the weather, and family traditions.

Food gives us **energy** to work and play, helps us **grow**, **repairs** our body when we get hurt, and **protects** us from diseases. But not all foods do all these jobs. To stay healthy, we need to know what our food contains and eat it thoughtfully.

In this chapter, we will learn about the **nutrients** in food, how to test foods for some of them, what a **balanced diet** is, what happens when nutrients are missing, and how to eat **mindfully**.

## 3.2 What are Nutrients?
> **Definition: Nutrients**
> The substances in food that our body needs for energy, growth, repair and protection are called **nutrients**.

The main nutrients are **carbohydrates**, **proteins**, **fats**, **vitamins** and **minerals**. Besides these, our body also needs **dietary fibre** and **water**, though they are not counted as nutrients.

Most foods contain more than one nutrient, but usually one of them is present in a larger amount. For example, rice is rich in carbohydrates, while dal is rich in proteins.

## 3.3 Types of Nutrients and their Work
### Carbohydrates – energy-giving foods
Carbohydrates give us **energy**. The main carbohydrates in our food are **starch** and **sugars**.
**Sources:** rice, wheat, maize, millets (ragi, jowar, bajra), potato, sweet potato, sugarcane, jaggery, fruits like banana and mango.

### Fats – stored energy
Fats also give **energy** – in fact, much more energy than the same amount of carbohydrates. Extra fat is stored in the body and used when needed. Fats also keep us warm.
**Sources:** ghee, butter, oil, cream, nuts, groundnuts, cheese, egg yolk.

SAY: buddy-think | Careful, Aarav! Too much fat is stored in the body and can cause health problems. We need fats, but only in small amounts.

### Proteins – body-building foods
Proteins help our body **grow** and **repair** damaged parts. Muscles, skin, hair and nails are all built from proteins. Growing children need plenty of proteins.
**Sources from plants:** pulses (dals), beans, peas, soybean, groundnuts.
**Sources from animals:** milk, paneer, curd, eggs, fish, meat.

### Vitamins – protective foods
Vitamins are needed only in **small amounts**, but they are very important. They **protect** us from diseases and keep our eyes, bones, teeth, gums and skin healthy. Vitamins are named by letters: **A**, **B**, **C**, **D**, **E** and **K**.

| Vitamin | Needed for | Some sources |
| Vitamin A | Healthy eyes and skin | Carrot, papaya, mango, green leafy vegetables, milk |
| Vitamin B (a group) | Using energy from food; healthy nerves | Whole grains, pulses, milk, eggs |
| Vitamin C | Healthy gums; helps fight infections and heal wounds | Amla, orange, lemon, guava, tomato |
| Vitamin D | Strong bones and teeth | Sunlight on our skin, milk, eggs, fish |

> **Did you know?**
> Our skin makes **vitamin D** when it is exposed to sunlight. So playing outdoors in the morning sun is good for your bones!

### Minerals – protective foods
Minerals are also needed in small amounts for good health.

| Mineral | Needed for | Some sources |
| Calcium | Strong bones and teeth | Milk, curd, ragi, green leafy vegetables, sesame |
| Iron | Making blood (haemoglobin) | Green leafy vegetables, jaggery, dates, pulses, meat |
| Iodine | Proper working of the thyroid gland | Iodised salt, seafood |
| Phosphorus | Bones and teeth | Milk, pulses, nuts |

### Dietary fibre and water
**Dietary fibre** (also called **roughage**) does not give nutrients, but it helps food move smoothly through our body and prevents **constipation**. Sources: whole grains, fruits, vegetables, pulses and salad.

**Water** helps the body take in nutrients, removes wastes through urine and sweat, and keeps our body temperature right. We should drink plenty of clean water every day.

## 3.4 Testing Food for Nutrients
How can we find out which nutrients a food contains? Simple tests can help. Do these with your teacher.

> **Activity 3.1: Test for starch**
> **You need:** small pieces of potato, a slice of bread, a little cooked rice, a piece of cucumber, a dropper and **dilute iodine solution**.
> **What to do:** Put each food on a separate plate. Add two or three drops of iodine solution to each.
> **Observation:** Potato, bread and rice turn **blue-black**. Cucumber does not.
> **Conclusion:** A **blue-black** colour shows that the food contains **starch**.

FIG: c6-sci-3-starch-test.svg | Fig. 3.2: Testing for starch – iodine turns a potato slice blue-black

> **Activity 3.2: Test for fat**
> **You need:** a few groundnuts, a little ghee or butter, a piece of boiled potato and some sheets of plain paper.
> **What to do:** Rub each food on a separate piece of paper. Let the paper dry. Hold each paper up against the light.

> **Conclusion:** A **translucent (oily) patch** on paper shows that the food contains **fat**.

> **Activity 3.3: Test for protein**

## 3.5 A Balanced Diet
No single food has all the nutrients in the right amounts. That is why we must eat a **variety** of foods.

> **Definition: Balanced diet**
> A diet that contains **all the nutrients**, along with enough **roughage** and **water**, in the **right amounts** for the needs of our body, is called a **balanced diet**.

A useful way to plan a balanced meal is to think of your plate:
- About **half** the plate: **vegetables and fruits** (vitamins, minerals and fibre).
- About **a quarter**: **cereals and millets** – rice, roti, ragi, jowar (carbohydrates).
- About **a quarter**: **pulses, milk, paneer, eggs, fish or meat** (proteins).
- A little **oil, ghee or nuts** (fats), and a glass of **water**.

FIG: c6-sci-3-plate.svg | Fig. 3.4: A balanced plate – half vegetables and fruits, a quarter cereals and millets, a quarter protein foods

The amount of food we need depends on our **age**, **body size**, **work** and whether we are healthy or ill. A growing child, a farmer working in the fields and an athlete need more energy than a person who sits at a desk all day.

## 3.6 Deficiency Diseases
If a person does not get enough of a particular nutrient for a long time, they may develop a **deficiency disease**.

> **Definition: Deficiency disease**
> A disease caused by the lack of a nutrient in the diet over a long time is called a **deficiency disease**.

| Nutrient lacking | Disease | Symptoms | Foods that help |
| Vitamin A | Night blindness | Poor vision in dim light and at night | Carrot, papaya, mango, green leafy vegetables, milk |
| Vitamin B1 | Beriberi | Weak muscles, very little energy | Whole grains, pulses, unpolished rice |
| Vitamin C | Scurvy | Bleeding gums, wounds take long to heal | Amla, citrus fruits, guava, tomato |
| Vitamin D | Rickets | Soft, bent bones | Sunlight, milk, eggs, fish |
| Calcium | Weak bones and teeth | Bones break easily, tooth decay | Milk, curd, ragi, green leafy vegetables |
| Iodine | Goitre | Swelling of the thyroid gland in the neck | Iodised salt, seafood |
| Iron | Anaemia | Tiredness, pale skin, breathlessness | Green leafy vegetables, jaggery, dates, pulses |

Lack of proteins and energy-giving foods in young children can cause severe weakness and stop them from growing properly. Deficiency diseases can be **prevented** by eating a balanced diet.

## 3.7 Mindful Eating
**Mindful eating** means paying attention to **what**, **how** and **how much** we eat.

## 4.1 Introduction
Have you ever seen a small, colourful object stuck to the door of a refrigerator, holding a list or a photograph in place? Or a pencil box whose lid shuts by itself with a soft "click"? Have you noticed how a few iron pins jump up and cling to certain objects? All of these work because of **magnets**.

SAY: riya-wow | Our fridge has a little mango magnet that never falls off! How does it stick without any glue?
SAY: buddy | Great question, Riya! The fridge door is made of iron or steel, and magnets pull iron towards them. Let's find out more about this amazing power!

There is an interesting old story about how magnets were discovered. It is said that long ago, in a region called Magnesia in ancient Greece, a shepherd named **Magnes** was grazing his sheep on a hillside. His walking stick had an iron tip, and his sandals had iron nails. As he walked over a certain black rock, he felt his stick and sandals being pulled towards the rock and sticking to it! That rock was a natural magnet. Today we call it **magnetite**. Whether the story is exactly true or not, magnetite really is found in nature, and people in many parts of the world, including India, China and Greece, have known about its strange power for thousands of years.

In this chapter, we will explore magnets – what they attract and what they do not, where their strength is greatest, how they help us find directions, how two magnets behave with each other, and how we can make a magnet of our own.

> **Definition: Magnet**
> A **magnet** is an object that attracts (pulls towards itself) objects made of certain materials, such as **iron**, **nickel** and **cobalt**.

### Natural and artificial magnets
Magnets found in nature, like magnetite (also called **lodestone**), are called **natural magnets**. They are usually irregular in shape and not very strong.
Magnets made by people from pieces of iron, steel or special alloys are called **artificial magnets**. They can be made in many useful shapes and are much stronger. Figure 4.1 shows some common shapes.

FIG: c6-sci-4-shapes.svg | Fig. 4.1: Magnets of different shapes – (a) bar, (b) horseshoe (U-shaped), (c) ring, (d) cylindrical and (e) needle magnet

## 4.2 Magnetic and Non-magnetic Materials
Does a magnet attract everything? Let us find out with a simple activity.

> **Activity 4.1: Which objects does a magnet attract?**
> **You need:** a bar magnet and a collection of objects – an iron nail, a steel spoon, a coin, a pencil, an eraser, a plastic scale, a paper clip, a piece of cloth, a key, a rubber band, an aluminium foil and a glass marble.
> **What to do:** Bring the magnet close to each object one by one. Note whether the object is pulled towards the magnet and sticks to it.

| Iron nail | Iron | Yes |
| Paper clip | Iron/steel | Yes |
| Steel spoon | Steel (mostly iron) | Yes (most steel spoons) |
| Key | Iron or brass | Depends on the metal |

You will find that the magnet attracts only some of the objects. > **Definition: Magnetic and non-magnetic materials**
> Materials that are attracted by a magnet are called **magnetic materials** – for example, **iron, nickel, cobalt** and steel.
> Materials that are not attracted by a magnet are called **non-magnetic materials** – for example, wood, plastic, rubber, paper, glass, cloth, aluminium and copper.

> **Remember:** "Metal" does not always mean "magnetic". A magnet attracts iron, nickel and cobalt, but not most other metals like aluminium, copper or gold.

> **Example 4.1**

## 4.3 Poles of a Magnet
Is the attraction of a magnet equally strong everywhere on it? Let us investigate.

> **Activity 4.2: Where is a magnet strongest?**
> Spread some **iron filings** (tiny bits of iron) on a sheet of paper. Place a bar magnet on them and gently roll it over. Lift the magnet and look at it carefully.
> **Observation:** Most of the iron filings stick near the two **ends** of the magnet. Very few stick to the middle.

FIG: c6-sci-4-filings.svg | Fig. 4.2: Iron filings cling mostly near the ends of a bar magnet

The two ends of a magnet, where its attraction is strongest, are called its **poles**. Every magnet has **two poles** – a **North pole (N)** and a **South pole (S)**. This is true for all magnets, whatever their shape: a horseshoe magnet has its poles at the two ends of the "U", a ring magnet has them on its two flat faces, and a cylindrical magnet has them at the two round ends.

> **Did you know?**
> If you break a bar magnet into two pieces, you do not get a separate North pole and South pole. Each piece becomes a complete magnet with **both** a North and a South pole! However many times you break it, every piece has two poles.

SAY: aarav-wow | So if I break a magnet into ten pieces, I get ten tiny magnets?
SAY: buddy | Exactly! Each little piece will have its own North pole and South pole.

## 4.4 Finding Directions using a Magnet
People have used magnets to find their way for centuries. How? Let us find out.

> **Activity 4.3: A freely suspended magnet**
> Tie a thread around the middle of a bar magnet so that it balances. Hang it from a wooden stand (not an iron one) where there is no other magnet or iron object nearby. Let it come to rest. Mark the direction of its ends on the floor.
> Now turn the magnet gently and let it settle again. Repeat this a few times.
> **Observation:** Every time, the magnet comes to rest in the **same direction** – roughly **north–south**.

A freely suspended magnet always comes to rest in the **north–south direction**. The end of the magnet that points towards the **geographic north** is called its **north-seeking pole** or **North pole**; the other end, pointing south, is its **South pole**. This is how the poles of a magnet got their names.

### The magnetic compass
A **compass** uses this property. It is a small box with a glass cover, containing a light **magnetised needle** that can turn freely on a pin. The North pole of the needle is usually painted red. A circular dial shows the directions – North, South, East and West.

FIG: c6-sci-4-compass.svg | Fig. 4.3: A magnetic compass – the red end of the needle points towards north

To use a compass, place it on a flat surface away from iron objects and wait for the needle to stop. Then turn the box gently until the "N" on the dial lines up with the red end of the needle. All the other directions can now be read from the dial.

SAY: riya | On our school trek, the guide used a compass to lead us back to the camp!
SAY: buddy | That's how hikers, sailors and pilots find their way. The needle always settles in the north–south direction.

> **Did you know?**
> Long before modern instruments, sailors and travellers used **lodestones** and magnetised needles to find directions at sea, especially on cloudy nights when the stars could not be seen. In ancient China, a spoon carved from lodestone was placed on a smooth bronze plate – it always turned to point **south**! This was one of the earliest compasses.

## 4.5 Attraction and Repulsion between Magnets
What happens when the poles of two magnets are brought close to each other?

> **Activity 4.4: Two magnets meet**
> Take two bar magnets with their poles marked. Place one on a table. Bring the North pole of the second magnet close to the North pole of the first one. Then try North near South, and South near South.
> **Observation:** When the **same poles** face each other (N–N or S–S), the magnets **push away** from each other. When **different poles** face each other (N–S), they **pull together** and stick.

FIG: c6-sci-4-attract-repel.svg | Fig. 4.4: Unlike poles attract each other; like poles repel each other

From this activity we learn a basic law of magnets:
> **Remember:** **Like poles repel; unlike poles attract.**
> N and N repel. S and S repel. N and S attract.

### The sure test for a magnet
Suppose you have two identical-looking iron bars, and only one is a magnet. How can you tell which one? If the bar **attracts** a pole of your magnet, it could be either a magnet (unlike pole) or just a piece of iron (iron is attracted by both poles). But if it **repels** one pole of your magnet, it **must** be a magnet – only a magnet can repel a magnet.
> **Remember:** **Repulsion is the sure test of magnetism.**

SAY: aarav-think | I tried to push two North poles together, but they kept sliding away – as if they don't want to be friends!
SAY: buddy-wow | Ha ha! That's repulsion – like poles repel. Turn one magnet around and – click! – unlike poles attract.

> **Example 4.2**
> A boy brings the North pole of a magnet near one end of an iron bar, and the bar is attracted. Can he conclude that the bar is a magnet?
> **Solution:** No. A plain iron bar is also attracted by a magnet. He should bring the North pole near the same end again and check the other end too. If any end is **repelled**, the bar is a magnet.

## 4.6 Making Your Own Magnet
You can turn an ordinary piece of iron into a magnet yourself!

> **Activity 4.5: Make a magnet by stroking**
> **You need:** a bar magnet and a long iron nail (or an iron strip or a large sewing needle).
> **What to do:** Place the nail on a table. Put one pole of the bar magnet near the **head** of the nail and slide it along to the **tip**. Then lift the magnet high above the nail and bring it back to the head. Repeat this **30–40 times**, always moving the magnet in the **same direction** and using the **same pole**.
> **Test it:** Bring the nail near some iron pins. Do they stick to it? Your nail has become a magnet!

FIG: c6-sci-4-magnetise.svg | Fig. 4.5: Making a magnet by stroking an iron nail in one direction

Magnets made in this way are **temporary** – they lose their magnetism after some time. Factories make stronger, longer-lasting magnets using electricity.

> **Activity 4.6: Make your own compass**
> Magnetise a sewing needle using Activity 4.5. Push it through a small piece of cork (or stick it on a flat piece of thermocol) and float it in a bowl of water. Keep the bowl away from iron objects.
> **Observation:** The needle turns and comes to rest in the north–south direction. You have made a working compass!

## 4.7 Uses of Magnets
Magnets are used all around us:
- **At home:** refrigerator doors and fridge magnets, magnetic pencil boxes and bag clasps, cupboard latches.
- **Electronics:** speakers, earphones, microphones, electric motors in fans and mixers, and generators that produce electricity.
- **Finding directions:** compasses used by sailors, pilots, soldiers and trekkers.
- **Industry:** large **electromagnets** fitted to cranes lift heavy scrap iron; magnets separate iron pieces from waste.
- **Medicine:** **MRI** machines in hospitals use powerful magnets to take pictures of the inside of the body.
- **Transport:** some fast trains, called **maglev** (magnetic levitation) trains, float above the track using magnets, so there is no rubbing and they can go very fast.
- **Toys and games:** magnetic building blocks, puzzles and fishing games.

## 4.8 Taking Care of Magnets
Magnets can **lose their magnetism** if they are:
- **heated** strongly,
- **hammered** or **dropped** from a height,
- kept carelessly, loosely in a box.

To keep bar magnets strong, store them **in pairs** with **unlike poles side by side**, separated by a piece of wood, with two pieces of soft iron, called **keepers**, placed across their ends (Fig. 4.6). Horseshoe magnets should be kept with a piece of iron across their poles.

FIG: c6-sci-4-storage.svg | Fig. 4.6: Storing bar magnets in a pair with keepers

SAY: buddy | Yes, Aarav. Dropping, hammering and heating make magnets lose their strength. Store them carefully with keepers.

> **Remember:** Keep magnets away from mobile phones, computers, televisions, cassettes and bank cards, as magnets can damage them.

## Summary
- A **magnet** attracts objects made of **magnetic materials** such as iron, nickel and cobalt.
- Materials not attracted by magnets are **non-magnetic** – wood, plastic, glass, rubber, aluminium and copper.
- **Natural magnets** (magnetite or lodestone) occur in nature; **artificial magnets** are made in shapes such as bar, horseshoe, ring, cylinder and needle.
- The attraction of a magnet is strongest at its **poles**. Every magnet has two poles – **North** and **South**.
- A freely suspended magnet always rests in the **north–south** direction. A **compass** uses this property to find directions.
- **Like poles repel; unlike poles attract.** **Repulsion** is the sure test of a magnet.
- A magnet can be made by **stroking** an iron object with a magnet in one direction.
- Magnets lose their magnetism when heated, hammered or dropped; they should be stored with **keepers**.

## 5.1 Introduction
How tall are you? How far is your school from your home? How long is your classroom? To answer such questions, we need to **measure**.

Long ago, people in different parts of the world measured length using parts of their own body. In India, people used the **angul** (the width of a finger), the **mutthi** (a fist), the **balisht** or **hand span** (from the tip of the thumb to the tip of the little finger of a stretched hand), and the **hasta** or **cubit** (from the elbow to the tip of the middle finger). Distances were measured in **paces** (steps).

In this chapter, we will learn why standard units are needed, how to measure length correctly, and how to describe **motion** – the movement of objects around us.

## 5.2 What is Measurement?
> **Definition: Measurement**
> **Measurement** is the comparison of an unknown quantity with a known, fixed quantity. The known fixed quantity is called a **unit**.

The result of a measurement has two parts – a **number** and a **unit**. If the length of a table is 2 metres, then "2" is the number and "metre" is the unit. Writing just "2" tells us nothing – 2 what? 2 centimetres, 2 metres or 2 kilometres?

## 5.3 Standard Units
To make measurements the same everywhere, people agreed on **standard units**. A standard unit does not change with the person or the place.

In 1790s France, scientists created the **metric system**. Today, scientists all over the world use the **International System of Units**, called **SI units**.

> **Definition: SI unit of length**
> The SI unit of length is the **metre**, written as **m**.

For measuring small lengths, we use **centimetres (cm)** and **millimetres (mm)**. For long distances, we use **kilometres (km)**.

| Unit | Relation | Used to measure |
| Millimetre (mm) | 10 mm = 1 cm | Thickness of a coin, length of an ant |
| Centimetre (cm) | 100 cm = 1 m | Length of a pencil, width of a book |
| Metre (m) | 1 m = 100 cm | Length of a room, height of a tree |
| Kilometre (km) | 1 km = 1000 m | Distance between towns |

> **Example 5.1**
> (a) Convert 3.5 m into centimetres. (b) Convert 2500 m into kilometres.
> **Solution:** (a) 1 m = 100 cm, so 3.5 m = 3.5 × 100 cm = **350 cm**.
> (b) 1000 m = 1 km, so 2500 m = 2500 ÷ 1000 km = **2.5 km**.

SAY: riya-happy | So the metre is like a hand span that everyone in the world has agreed to share!

## 5.4 Measuring Length Correctly
We use different tools for different lengths – a **ruler** (scale) for short lengths, a **measuring tape** for cloth or the size of a room, and a **metre scale** for longer objects.

Follow these rules to measure correctly with a ruler:
1. Place the ruler **along** the length of the object, touching it.
2. Line up one end of the object with the **zero mark** of the ruler.
3. If the ruler's edge is broken or worn, start at a clear full mark, such as 1 cm, and **subtract** it from the final reading.
4. Keep your eye **directly above** the point where you take the reading. Looking from the side gives a wrong reading.

FIG: c6-sci-5-ruler.svg | Fig. 5.2: Reading a ruler – keep your eye directly above the mark (position B), not to one side (A or C)

> **Example 5.2**

> **Solution:** Length = 12.5 cm − 2.0 cm = **10.5 cm**.

## 5.5 Motion and Rest
Look around you. A bus moves on the road, a bird flies across the sky, a ceiling fan turns and a child swings in the park. A tree, a house and an electric pole stay where they are.

> **Definition: Motion and rest**
> An object is said to be in **motion** if its **position changes with time**. An object is at **rest** if its position does not change with time.

How do we know an object has moved? We compare its position with something that stays fixed, such as a tree or a building. This is called a **reference point**.

## 5.6 Types of Motion
Objects move in different ways. We can group motion into three main types.

### Linear (rectilinear) motion
When an object moves along a **straight line**, its motion is called **linear** or **rectilinear motion**.
**Examples:** a train on a straight track, soldiers in a march-past, an apple falling from a tree, a ball rolling straight along the floor.

### Circular motion
When an object moves along a **circular path**, its motion is called **circular motion**. The object stays at the same distance from a fixed centre.
**Examples:** the tip of a moving fan blade, a giant wheel, a merry-go-round, a stone tied to a thread and whirled around.

### Oscillatory (periodic) motion
When an object moves **to and fro** (back and forth) about a fixed point, again and again, its motion is called **oscillatory motion**.
**Examples:** a swing, a pendulum of a clock, a plucked sitar string, the branch of a tree swaying in the wind.

FIG: c6-sci-5-motion-types.svg | Fig. 5.4: Types of motion – (a) linear, (b) circular and (c) oscillatory

### More than one type of motion
Some objects show more than one type of motion at the same time. A **bicycle wheel** turns round and round (circular motion) while the bicycle moves forward along the road (linear motion). The **needle of a sewing machine** moves up and down while the wheel of the machine turns.

| Object | Type of motion |

| A swing | Oscillatory |
| A ball rolling down a slope | Linear (the ball also rotates) |
| A plucked guitar string | Oscillatory |
| A spinning top | Circular (spinning) |

## 6.3 Properties of Materials
Materials differ from one another in many ways. These features are called their **properties**. Let us study some important properties.

### Appearance: lustre
Freshly cut or polished metals like gold, silver, copper and iron **shine**. This shine is called **lustre**, and such materials are called **lustrous**. Wood, paper, cloth and clay do not shine – they are **non-lustrous** (dull).
Metals often lose their shine over time because air and moisture form a dull layer on them. Rubbing them with sandpaper shows the shine again.

> **Activity 6.2: Find the shine**
> Take an old iron nail, a copper coin and a piece of wood. Rub each with sandpaper. What do you see? The metals become shiny where they are rubbed; the wood does not.

### Hardness
Some materials are difficult to press or scratch – they are **hard**, like iron, stone and diamond. Others can be pressed, scratched or bent easily – they are **soft**, like cotton, sponge, rubber and wax.

> **Example 6.1**
> Riya scratches a piece of chalk, a candle, a steel spoon and a piece of wood with an iron nail. Which is the hardest?
> **Solution:** The steel spoon is the hardest – it is very difficult to scratch. Chalk and wax scratch very easily, so they are soft.

### Solubility in water
> **Activity 6.3: Does it dissolve?**
> **You need:** six glasses of water, and a spoonful each of sugar, salt, sand, chalk powder, sawdust and lemon juice.
> **What to do:** Add one substance to each glass and stir well. Wait for a few minutes and observe.
> **Observation:** Sugar, salt and lemon juice disappear in the water – they **dissolve**. Sand, chalk powder and sawdust do not dissolve; they settle down or float.

FIG: c6-sci-6-solubility.svg | Fig. 6.1: Sugar dissolves in water and disappears; sand does not dissolve and settles at the bottom

> **Definition: Soluble and insoluble**
> Substances that dissolve completely in water are **soluble** in water. Substances that do not dissolve in water, even after stirring, are **insoluble** in water.

Water dissolves so many substances that it is sometimes called a "universal solvent".

**Liquids in water:** Some liquids, such as vinegar, lemon juice and milk, mix completely with water. Others, such as coconut oil, mustard oil and kerosene, do not mix – they form a separate layer on top.

**Gases in water:** Some gases dissolve in water. **Oxygen** dissolved in water allows fish and other water animals to breathe.

SAY: buddy | That's right, Riya! A small amount of oxygen from the air dissolves in water. Fish take it in through their gills.

### Floating and sinking
Objects such as dry leaves, cork, a plastic ball and a piece of wood **float** on water. Objects such as a stone, a coin and an iron nail **sink**.

> **Remember:** Whether an object floats does not depend only on how heavy it is. A huge wooden log floats on a river, but a tiny iron pin sinks! It depends on the material and the shape of the object.

### Transparency
Can you see through a material? Based on this, materials are of three types.

> **Definition: Transparent, translucent and opaque**
> - **Transparent** materials let light pass through them completely, so we can see clearly through them. Examples: clear glass, clean water, air, some plastics.
> - **Translucent** materials let only some light pass through, so we can see through them only partly, not clearly. Examples: butter paper, frosted glass, oiled paper, a thin cloth.
> - **Opaque** materials do not let light pass through them at all, so we cannot see through them. Examples: wood, metal, cardboard, a brick wall.

FIG: c6-sci-6-transparency.svg | Fig. 6.2: Looking at a torch through (a) clear glass – transparent, (b) butter paper – translucent and (c) cardboard – opaque

> **Activity 6.4: See-through test**
> Hold a lit torch behind a sheet of clear plastic, a sheet of butter paper and a piece of cardboard, one at a time. Look at the torch from the other side. Through which can you see the torch clearly, partly, or not at all?

### Heat: conductors and insulators
If you touch a steel spoon kept in hot tea, the handle soon feels hot. A wooden or plastic spoon does not get hot so quickly. Materials that let heat pass through them easily, like metals, are called **good conductors of heat**. Materials that do not let heat pass easily, like wood, plastic and cloth, are **poor conductors** (insulators) of heat.

| Light | Transparent: glass, water; Translucent: butter paper | Opaque: wood, metal, cardboard |
| Heat | Good conductors: metals | Poor conductors: wood, plastic |

### Why grouping helps
Grouping materials by their properties makes it easy to:
- **Choose the right material for a job.** Cooking pots are made of metal because metals conduct heat well; their handles are made of plastic or wood so that we can hold them safely. Windows are made of transparent glass to let light in. Raincoats are made of waterproof plastic.
- **Find things quickly.** Shopkeepers keep grains, pulses, oils and soaps in separate groups.
- **Study materials** in an organised way.

## 7.2 Hot and Cold: Can We Trust Our Touch?

> **Caution:** Never test very hot water, a hot iron or a stove with your hand. Touching very hot things can burn you.

## 7.3 What is Temperature?
> **Definition: Temperature**
> **Temperature** is a measure of how **hot** or **cold** an object is. A hotter object has a higher temperature than a colder one.

Temperature is measured with an instrument called a **thermometer**. The unit of temperature commonly used in daily life is the **degree Celsius**, written as **°C**. It is named after the Swedish scientist Anders Celsius.

On the Celsius scale:
- Pure water **freezes** (turns into ice) at **0 °C**.
- Pure water **boils** at **100 °C** (at normal air pressure).
- The average normal temperature of the human body is about **37 °C**.

The SI unit of temperature is the **kelvin (K)**. To change degrees Celsius into kelvin, we add 273 (approximately). So 0 °C is about 273 K.

FIG: c6-sci-7-scale.svg | Fig. 7.2: Some temperatures on the Celsius scale

## 7.4 The Clinical Thermometer
A thermometer used to measure the temperature of the **human body** is called a **clinical thermometer**. Doctors and nurses use it, and many families keep one at home.

The traditional clinical thermometer is a long, narrow glass tube with a **bulb** at one end. The bulb contains a shiny liquid (mercury). When the bulb touches a warm body, the liquid expands and rises up a very thin tube inside. A **scale** marked on the glass shows the temperature.

### Main features
- **Range:** a clinical thermometer reads from **35 °C to 42 °C**. Our body temperature normally stays within this range, even when we are ill.
- **Kink:** just above the bulb, the tube has a small bend called a **kink**. It stops the liquid from falling back into the bulb on its own when the thermometer is taken out of the mouth, so that we can read it calmly.
- **Divisions:** each small division on the scale usually stands for **0.1 °C**.

FIG: c6-sci-7-clinical.svg | Fig. 7.3: A clinical thermometer – the kink stops the liquid from falling back

### How to use a clinical thermometer
1. Wash the thermometer with an antiseptic solution or soap and water.
2. Hold it firmly and give it a few gentle jerks so that the liquid falls **below 35 °C**.
3. Place the bulb **under the tongue** (or in the armpit) for about **one minute**.
4. Take it out and read it, holding it at eye level. Do not hold it by the bulb.
5. Wash it again after use.

> **Remember:** "Normal" body temperature is about 37 °C on average, but it can be slightly higher or lower in different healthy people and at different times of the day. A temperature well above normal is called a **fever**.

SAY: buddy | Yes, Aarav! Our body works best in a very narrow range of temperatures. That's why even a small rise matters.

### Digital thermometers
Today, many homes and hospitals use **digital thermometers**. They have a small sensor at the tip and show the temperature as numbers on a screen, often with a beep when the reading is ready. They are easy to read and safer, because they contain no glass or mercury. **Infrared thermometers** can even measure body temperature from the forehead without touching.

> **Mercury safety**
> Mercury is a **poisonous** substance. If a mercury thermometer breaks, do not touch the shiny drops. Tell an adult at once. Because of this danger, many countries are replacing mercury thermometers with digital ones.

## 7.5 The Laboratory Thermometer
A clinical thermometer cannot be used to measure the temperature of boiling water or ice – it would read only 35 °C to 42 °C and could break if heated too much. For other objects, we use a **laboratory thermometer**.

| Feature | Clinical thermometer | Laboratory thermometer |
| Used for | Human body temperature | Temperature of water, liquids and other objects |
| Range | 35 °C to 42 °C | About −10 °C to 110 °C |
| Kink | Has a kink | No kink |
| When to read | After taking it out | While it is still in the liquid |
| Shaking before use | Yes | No |

### How to use a laboratory thermometer
1. Hold the thermometer **upright** (vertical), not tilted.
2. The bulb should be **completely surrounded** by the substance – for example, dipped in the water.
3. The bulb should **not touch** the bottom or the sides of the container.
4. Wait until the liquid in the tube stops moving.
5. Read the temperature **while the thermometer is still in** the water, because there is no kink – the level begins to change as soon as it is taken out.

FIG: c6-sci-7-lab.svg | Fig. 7.4: Using a laboratory thermometer – (a) the right way, (b) bulb touching the bottom (wrong) and (c) tilted (wrong)

## 7.6 Reading a Thermometer Correctly
1. First find out the **range** of the thermometer and what each small division stands for.
2. Keep your **eye level** with the top of the liquid column. Looking from above or below gives a wrong reading.
3. Note the number, and do not forget the unit, °C.

> **Example 7.1**
> On a laboratory thermometer, there are 10 small divisions between the 20 °C and 30 °C marks. The liquid stands 4 small divisions above 20 °C. What is the temperature?
> **Solution:** 10 divisions stand for 30 − 20 = 10 °C, so each division = 1 °C. The temperature is 20 °C + 4 × 1 °C = **24 °C**.

> **Example 7.2**
> A clinical thermometer has 10 small divisions between 37 °C and 38 °C. The reading is 6 divisions above 37 °C. Does the person have a fever?
> **Solution:** Each division = 1 ÷ 10 = 0.1 °C. Temperature = 37 + 6 × 0.1 = **37.6 °C**. This is slightly above the average normal temperature, so the person may have a mild fever. A doctor should be consulted if it continues.

Water is found in three forms – solid, liquid and gas. In this chapter, we will learn about these three states of water, how water changes from one state to another, what makes water dry up faster, why evaporation cools things, and how water travels around the Earth in the **water cycle**.

## 8.2 The Three States of Water
Water exists in three **states**:
- **Solid** – ice and snow. Solids have a fixed shape and a fixed volume.
- **Liquid** – the water we drink. Liquids have a fixed volume but no fixed shape; they take the shape of their container and can flow.
- **Gas** – water vapour. A gas has neither a fixed shape nor a fixed volume; it spreads out to fill all the space available. Water vapour is **invisible**.

FIG: c6-sci-8-states.svg | Fig. 8.1: The three states of water – (a) solid: ice, (b) liquid: water and (c) gas: water vapour (invisible) rising from boiling water

> **Remember:** The white "steam" you see coming out of a kettle is not water vapour itself. Water vapour is invisible. When it meets the cooler air, it changes into tiny droplets of liquid water, which we can see as a white mist.

## 8.3 Changes of State
Water can change from one state to another when it is **heated** or **cooled**.

> **Definition: Melting**
> The change of a **solid into a liquid** on heating is called **melting**. Ice melts at **0 °C**.

### Freezing
When water is cooled in the freezer of a refrigerator, it turns into ice.
> **Definition: Freezing**
> The change of a **liquid into a solid** on cooling is called **freezing**. Water freezes at **0 °C**.

### Evaporation
> **Definition: Evaporation**
> The change of a **liquid into its vapour (gas)** is called **evaporation**. Water evaporates at all temperatures – even at room temperature – from its surface.

When water is heated strongly, it **boils** at 100 °C, changing into vapour very quickly from all through the liquid.

## 8.4 What Makes Evaporation Faster?

The rate of evaporation increases with:
1. **Larger surface area:** water spread out over a larger area evaporates faster. That is why we spread out clothes to dry instead of leaving them folded.
2. **Higher temperature:** water evaporates faster in the hot sun than in the shade.
3. **Wind (moving air):** clothes dry faster on a windy day, because moving air carries away the water vapour.
4. **Less humidity:** the amount of water vapour already present in the air is called **humidity**. When the air is dry, water evaporates faster. On a humid, sticky day during the rainy season, clothes take a long time to dry.

## 8.5 Evaporation Causes Cooling
> **Activity 8.4: Feel the cool**
> Put a few drops of water (or, with your teacher's permission, a drop of hand sanitiser) on the back of your hand. Blow on it gently.
> **Observation:** The spot feels **cool** as the liquid dries.

When a liquid evaporates, it takes heat from its surroundings. So the surface it evaporates from becomes **cooler**. This is called the **cooling effect of evaporation**.

Examples from daily life:

- **The matka (clay pot):** a clay pot has tiny pores. Some water seeps out through them and evaporates from the outer surface, taking heat away. This keeps the water inside cool.
- **Sprinkling water:** people sprinkle water on the floor or the courtyard on hot evenings to cool the place.
- **Wet cloth:** wrapping a wet cloth around a water bottle keeps the water cooler.

## 8.6 The Water Cycle
The water on Earth keeps moving – from the oceans to the air, from the air to the land, and back to the oceans again. This continuous movement is called the **water cycle**.

1. **Evaporation:** the heat of the Sun evaporates water from oceans, rivers, lakes and the soil. Plants also give out water vapour through their leaves – this is called **transpiration**.
2. **Condensation:** as the water vapour rises high into the sky, it cools and condenses into tiny droplets of water. These droplets gather together to form **clouds**.
3. **Precipitation:** when the droplets join and grow too heavy, they fall to the Earth as **rain**. In very cold places they fall as **snow** or **hail**.
4. **Collection:** rainwater flows into streams, rivers, lakes and oceans, and some of it soaks into the ground to become **groundwater**. Then the cycle starts all over again.

FIG: c6-sci-8-water-cycle.svg | Fig. 8.5: The water cycle – evaporation, condensation, precipitation and collection

> **Did you know?**
> The water you drink today may once have been part of a glacier in the Himalayas, a cloud over the Bay of Bengal, or even a drink for a dinosaur! The same water has been going round and round in the water cycle for millions of years.

The water cycle is very important. It brings fresh rainwater to fill our rivers, lakes and wells, and to water our crops. Cutting down forests and polluting water disturb this cycle, so we must use water wisely and keep it clean.

All these people are **separating** the parts of a **mixture**. In this chapter, we will learn what mixtures are, why we separate them, and the many methods we use in daily life, in farms and in factories.

## 9.2 Mixtures and Why We Separate Them
> **Definition: Mixture**
> A **mixture** is made up of two or more substances mixed together, in which each substance keeps its own properties. Examples: rice and small stones, tea and tea leaves, sand and water, salt dissolved in water, the air around us.

> **Definition: Separation**
> Taking out one or more parts from a mixture is called **separation**.

We separate the parts of a mixture to:
- **remove unwanted or harmful parts** – stones from rice, mud from water;
- **obtain useful parts** – butter from curd, grain from stalks;
- **obtain a pure substance** – salt from sea water.

The method we choose depends on how the parts of the mixture **differ** – in size, shape, weight, colour, whether they dissolve in water, or whether a magnet attracts them.

## 9.3 Methods of Separating Solids from Solids
### Hand-picking
Small stones, husk or insects in rice, wheat and pulses can be seen easily and are **removed by hand**. This method is called **hand-picking**. It works only when the unwanted particles are bigger or have a different colour and there are only a few of them.

### Threshing
After harvesting, the stalks of wheat or paddy have the grain attached to them. **Beating** the stalks to free the grain is called **threshing**. Farmers beat bundles of stalks on a hard surface, let bullocks walk over them, or use **threshing machines**.

### Winnowing
After threshing, the grain is mixed with light **husk**. The farmer stands on a raised platform and lets the mixture fall slowly from a winnowing basket (soop). The **wind** blows the light husk away to form a separate heap, while the heavier grain falls straight down near the platform.

> **Definition: Winnowing**
> Separating the heavier and lighter parts of a mixture with the help of **wind** or blowing air is called **winnowing**.

FIG: c6-sci-9-winnowing.svg | Fig. 9.1: Winnowing – the wind blows the light husk away, while the heavy grain falls straight down

SAY: aarav-wow | So the wind does half the farmer's work for free!

### Sieving
At home, flour is passed through a **sieve** (channi). The fine flour passes through the small holes, while the bigger bran and any small stones stay on the sieve. At building sites, workers sieve sand to remove pebbles.

> **Definition: Sieving**
> Separating particles of **different sizes** by passing a mixture through a sieve is called **sieving**.

### Magnetic separation
If a mixture contains iron pins, nails or iron filings, a **magnet** can pull them out. In scrap yards, huge magnets fitted to cranes separate iron from other waste.

## 9.4 Methods of Separating Insoluble Solids from Liquids
### Sedimentation and decantation
> **Activity 9.1: Muddy water**
> **You need:** a glass of water, a spoonful of soil and an empty glass.
> **What to do:** Mix the soil into the water and stir. Leave the glass undisturbed for about 30 minutes.
> **Observation:** The heavier soil particles slowly **settle at the bottom**. The water above becomes clearer.
> Now gently tilt the glass and **pour** the clearer water into the empty glass, without disturbing the layer at the bottom.

> **Definition: Sedimentation and decantation**
> When the heavier insoluble particles in a liquid settle at the bottom, the process is called **sedimentation**. The settled layer is called the **sediment**.
> Gently pouring off the clear liquid on top, without disturbing the sediment, is called **decantation**.

We use sedimentation and decantation when we wash rice or dal: the dust and husk float or stay in the water, which is then poured off.

FIG: c6-sci-9-sedimentation.svg | Fig. 9.2: (a) Sedimentation – soil settles at the bottom; (b) decantation – the clearer water is poured off gently

### Filtration
The water after decantation may still contain very fine particles. To remove them, we use a **filter** – something with very tiny holes, such as **filter paper**, a fine cloth or a layer of sand.

> **Activity 9.2: Make a filter**
> Fold a round filter paper into a cone and place it in a funnel. Put the funnel on a glass. Slowly pour the decanted muddy water into the funnel.
> **Observation:** The clear water passes through the filter paper and collects in the glass. The fine mud stays on the filter paper.

> **Definition: Filtration**
> Separating an **insoluble solid** from a **liquid** by passing the mixture through a filter is called **filtration**. The clear liquid that passes through is called the **filtrate**; the solid left behind is called the **residue**.

FIG: c6-sci-9-filtration.svg | Fig. 9.3: Filtration using a funnel and filter paper

Straining tea is a kind of filtration – the strainer is the filter. Water purifiers at home also use filters.

> **Remember:** Filtration removes **insoluble** particles only. Germs and **dissolved** substances such as salt may pass through a simple filter. Water should be boiled or properly purified before drinking.

## 9.5 Separating a Dissolved Solid: Evaporation
How can we get back salt that has dissolved in water? Filtration will not work, because the dissolved salt passes through the filter paper along with the water.

> **Activity 9.3: Salt from salt water**
> With your teacher's help, pour some salt water into a steel bowl. Heat it gently on a burner (or keep it in strong sunlight for a few days) until all the water has gone.
> **Observation:** A white layer of **salt** is left behind in the bowl.

> **Definition: Evaporation (for separation)**
> Heating a solution so that the liquid changes into vapour and the dissolved solid is left behind is called separation by **evaporation**.

This is how common salt is made. Sea water is let into shallow **salt pans** along the coast – for example, in Gujarat and Tamil Nadu. The Sun's heat evaporates the water over many days, and the salt is collected and purified.

FIG: c6-sci-9-salt-pans.svg | Fig. 9.4: Salt pans – sea water evaporates in the sun, leaving salt behind

## 9.6 Churning
In many Indian homes, curd is churned with a wooden **mathani** to make butter. The fast, steady stirring separates the lighter **butter** (makhan), which floats on top, from the **buttermilk** (chhaachh). Dairies use machines to separate cream from milk in the same way.

> **Definition: Churning**
> Stirring a liquid fast so that its lighter parts separate from the heavier parts is called **churning**.

## 9.7 Using More Than One Method
Sometimes one method is not enough.

> **Example 9.1**
> How will you separate a mixture of sand, salt and water?
> **Solution:**
> 1. Let the sand settle, and **decant** most of the salt water. Then **filter** the rest. The sand stays on the filter paper as residue.
> 2. **Evaporate** the filtrate (salt water). The salt is left behind.

> **Example 9.2**
> A mixture contains iron filings, sand and sawdust. Suggest a way to separate them.
> **Solution:** Use a **magnet** to remove the iron filings. Then put the sand and sawdust in water – sawdust **floats** and can be skimmed off, while sand sinks. Decant the water and dry the sand.

In this chapter, we will learn the **characteristics** that all living things share, how living things grow and change in their **life cycles**, and what seeds need to start growing.

## 10.2 Characteristics of Living Things
Scientists have found that all living things show certain features together. Non-living things may show one or two of them, but never all of them.

### 1. Living things need food (nutrition)
All living things need **food**. Food gives them **energy** and helps them grow. Green plants make their own food in their leaves, using sunlight, water and air. Animals cannot make their own food – they eat plants or other animals.

### 2. Living things respire
All living things take in air and use **oxygen** to release energy from food. This process is called **respiration**. We breathe through our nose and lungs. Fish use gills, earthworms breathe through their moist skin, and plants exchange gases through tiny pores in their leaves called **stomata**.

### 3. Living things excrete
Living things produce **wastes** while carrying out their life processes. Getting rid of these wastes is called **excretion**. Humans remove wastes as urine and sweat. Plants give out extra water and gases, and some store wastes in old leaves that fall off.

### 4. Living things grow
A puppy grows into a dog, a chick into a hen and a small seed into a big tree. Living things **grow** from inside, by making new material. A pile of sand grows only when more sand is added from outside – that is not growth in the living sense.

### 5. Living things move
Animals **move** from place to place to find food, water and shelter, and to escape danger. Plants are fixed in one place, but their parts move – leaves turn towards light, roots grow towards water, flowers of some plants open in the morning and close at night, and climbers' tendrils wrap around supports.

### 6. Living things reproduce
All living things produce **young ones of their own kind**. This is called **reproduction**. Some animals, like cows, dogs and humans, give birth to babies. Others, like hens, frogs, snakes and fish, lay eggs. Most plants reproduce through seeds; some, like potato and rose, can also grow from parts of the plant.

### 7. Living things respond to stimuli
A change in the surroundings that makes a living thing react is called a **stimulus** (plural: **stimuli**). We pull our hand away quickly from a hot pan; our eyes blink when dust comes near; a cat runs when a dog barks. The leaves of the **touch-me-not** (chhui-mui) plant fold up when touched, and a sunflower turns to face the Sun.

### 8. Living things have a life span
Every living thing is **born**, lives for some time and then **dies**. This period is its **life span**. A housefly lives for a few weeks, a dog for about 10–15 years, a human for many decades, and some trees for hundreds of years.

> **Definition: Characteristics of living things**
> Living things need **food**, **respire**, **excrete**, **grow**, **move**, **reproduce**, **respond to stimuli** and have a definite **life span**.

## 10.3 Life Cycles
Living things change as they grow. The series of stages that a living thing passes through, from its beginning to becoming an adult that can produce young ones, is called its **life cycle**.

> **Definition: Life cycle**
> The series of changes a living thing goes through, from birth to adulthood and reproduction, is called its **life cycle**.

### Life cycle of a plant
A seed **germinates** (sprouts) when it gets water, air and warmth. A tiny root grows downwards and a shoot grows upwards. The seedling grows into a young plant, then an adult plant. The adult plant makes flowers, the flowers form fruits with seeds inside, and the seeds grow into new plants.

**seed → germination → seedling → adult plant → flowers → fruits with seeds → new seed**

### Life cycle of a frog
In the rainy season, frogs lay hundreds of eggs in ponds. A **tadpole** hatches from each egg. It lives in water, has a tail for swimming and breathes through gills, like a fish. Slowly its back legs grow, then its front legs. Its tail shrinks, and it becomes a **froglet**. Finally it becomes an adult **frog** that breathes with lungs and can live on land as well as in water.

**eggs → tadpole → tadpole with legs → froglet → adult frog**

FIG: c6-sci-10-frog-cycle.svg | Fig. 10.3: Life cycle of a frog

### Life cycle of a butterfly
A butterfly lays tiny **eggs** on a leaf. A **caterpillar** (larva) hatches and eats leaves all the time, growing quickly. Then it forms a hard case around itself and becomes a **pupa**. Inside the pupa, its body changes completely. Finally, an adult **butterfly** comes out.

**egg → caterpillar (larva) → pupa → adult butterfly**

FIG: c6-sci-10-butterfly-cycle.svg | Fig. 10.4: Life cycle of a butterfly

## 11.3 Types of Natural Resources
Natural resources are grouped into two types, depending on whether nature can replace them quickly.

But they will run out one day, and burning them pollutes the air.

- Moving air (wind) turns windmills and helps birds fly, seeds spread and clouds move.
- Air keeps the Earth warm enough for life and protects us from harmful rays of the Sun.

Smoke from vehicles, factories and burning garbage **pollutes** the air and causes breathing problems. Clean air is precious.

## 11.5 Water
Water covers about **71%** of the Earth's surface, yet most of it is **salty** sea water that we cannot drink or use for farming. Most of the fresh water is frozen as ice in glaciers and at the poles. Only a **very small part** of all the water on Earth is fresh water available in rivers, lakes and underground.

FIG: c6-sci-11-water.svg | Fig. 11.3: Out of all the water on Earth, only a tiny part is fresh water we can use

We need water for drinking, cooking, cleaning, farming, factories and producing electricity. Water is renewed by the **water cycle**, but rising demand, waste and pollution mean many places in India face water shortage, especially in summer.

> **Did you know?**
> A dripping tap can waste many litres of water in a single day. Closing the tap while brushing your teeth can save several litres every time!

## 11.6 Sunlight and Wind: Clean Energy
### Sunlight
The **Sun** is the main source of energy on Earth. Plants use sunlight to make food; all animals depend on plants. Sunlight also drives the water cycle and the winds.
We can use sunlight directly:
- **Solar cells** (solar panels) change sunlight into **electricity**. They are used on rooftops, street lights, water pumps and even satellites.
- **Solar cookers** and **solar water heaters** use the Sun's heat.

### Wind
Moving air has energy. Long ago, windmills were used to grind grain and pump water. Today, tall **wind turbines** turn the energy of the wind into **electricity**. India has many wind farms, especially in **Tamil Nadu** and **Gujarat**.

FIG: c6-sci-11-clean-energy.svg | Fig. 11.4: Clean energy – (a) solar panels on a roof and (b) wind turbines

Solar and wind energy are **renewable** and **clean** – they do not produce smoke and will never run out.

> **Example 11.1**
> A village has no electricity line, but it gets bright sunshine almost all year. Suggest a way to give the school light and fans.
> **Solution:** Fit **solar panels** on the school roof. They can produce electricity during the day and store some in batteries for use in the evening.

## 11.7 Soil and Forests
### Soil
**Soil** is the top layer of the Earth in which plants grow. It is formed very slowly, as rocks are broken down by heat, water and wind, and mixed with the remains of dead plants and animals (**humus**). It can take hundreds of years to form just a few centimetres of fertile soil! Soil gives plants water and nutrients and is home to earthworms, insects and tiny living things.
Wind and flowing water can carry away the top layer of soil – this is called **soil erosion**. Roots of plants hold the soil together and help prevent it.

### Forests
Forests are a treasure house. They give us:
- **oxygen** and clean air;
- **wood**, **fruits**, **honey**, **gum**, **medicinal plants** and **fodder**;
- **homes** for thousands of kinds of plants and animals;
- protection for soil from erosion, and help in bringing **rain** and keeping the place cool.

Many communities in India, especially tribal communities, live in and around forests and depend on them. When forests are cut down without care (**deforestation**), animals lose their homes, soil is washed away and rainfall may decrease.

> **Did you know?**
> In the 1970s, women in the villages of the Garhwal Himalayas hugged trees to stop them from being cut down. This was the famous **Chipko movement** ("chipko" means "to hug" or "to stick").

## 11.8 Using Resources Wisely
Natural resources are shared by all living things and by future generations. Using them carefully so that they last is called **conservation**. We can all help with the **3 Rs**:
- **Reduce:** use less – switch off lights and fans when not needed, close dripping taps, walk or cycle for short distances, take only as much food as you can eat.
- **Reuse:** use things again – write on both sides of paper, carry a cloth bag, reuse glass jars and boxes.
- **Recycle:** turn waste into new things – separate dry and wet waste so that paper, plastic and metal can be recycled, and turn kitchen waste into compost.

FIG: c6-sci-11-three-rs.svg | Fig. 11.5: The 3 Rs – reduce, reuse and recycle

We can also plant trees, harvest rainwater from rooftops, use public transport and use solar energy wherever possible.

## Summary
- **Natural resources** are materials and energy from nature that are useful to us.
- **Renewable resources** (sunlight, wind, air, water, forests) are replaced naturally; **non-renewable resources** (coal, petroleum, natural gas, minerals) are limited and take millions of years to form.
- Coal, petroleum and natural gas are **fossil fuels**.
- Air is a mixture of gases, mainly **nitrogen (about 78%)** and **oxygen (about 21%)**.
- Most of Earth's water is salty; only a very small part is usable fresh water.
- **Solar** and **wind** energy are clean and renewable.
- **Soil** forms very slowly; **forests** give us many useful things and protect soil and rainfall.
- We must **conserve** resources using the **3 Rs** – reduce, reuse and recycle.

People have watched the night sky for thousands of years. Ancient Indian astronomers such as **Aryabhata** and **Varahamihira** studied the movements of the Sun, the Moon and the planets very carefully. In this chapter, we will learn about stars, constellations, our solar system, other objects in space, and India's space missions.

## 12.2 Stars
> **Definition: Star**
> A **star** is a huge, extremely hot ball of glowing gases that gives out its **own light and heat**.

The **Sun** is a star – the star nearest to the Earth. It is about **15 crore kilometres** (150 million km) away. Other stars are so much farther away that their light takes years to reach us. That is why they look like tiny points of light, even though many of them are as big as the Sun or even bigger.

### Why can't we see stars in the daytime?
Stars are in the sky during the day too! But the Sun's light is so bright that we cannot see their faint light. At night, when the Sun's light is absent, the stars become visible.

### Why do stars twinkle?
Light from a star travels a very long way and then passes through the Earth's **air**, which is always moving and changing. This makes the starlight flicker, so the star seems to **twinkle**. Planets are much closer and look like tiny discs rather than points, so they shine more steadily.

### Do stars move?
If you watch the sky for a few hours, the stars seem to move slowly from **east to west**, like the Sun does. Actually, it is the **Earth** that spins (rotates) from west to east on its axis, which makes the stars appear to move the other way.

## 12.3 Constellations
Long ago, people noticed that some stars form shapes in the sky. They joined them with imaginary lines and gave them names.

> **Definition: Constellation**
> A **group of stars** that forms a recognisable shape or pattern in the sky is called a **constellation**.

Some well-known constellations:
- **Saptarishi (Ursa Major or the Great Bear):** seven bright stars shaped like a big ladle or a question mark. It is seen in the northern sky, especially in summer evenings.
- **Orion (the Hunter; in India, Mriga):** has three bright stars in a row forming the hunter's **belt**. It is seen in winter evenings.
- **Cassiopeia:** five bright stars forming the shape of a **W** or an **M**.

FIG: c6-sci-12-constellations.svg | Fig. 12.1: (a) Saptarishi, with its pointer stars leading to the Pole Star; (b) Orion and (c) Cassiopeia

### The Pole Star
One star in the northern sky hardly seems to move at all, because it lies almost directly above the Earth's North Pole. It is called the **Pole Star** (**Dhruva Tara**). Travellers and sailors used it to find the **north** direction at night. To find it, follow the two **pointer stars** at the end of Saptarishi's "ladle".

> **Activity 12.1: Find the Pole Star**
> On a clear night, face north and find Saptarishi. Imagine a line through its two pointer stars and extend it about five times the distance between them. The fairly bright star you reach is the Pole Star.

## 12.4 Our Solar System
> **Definition: Solar system**
> The **Sun** and all the objects that move around it – planets, their moons, asteroids, comets and meteoroids – together form the **solar system**.

The Sun is at the centre. It holds all the members of the solar system in their paths by its pull (gravity).

### The planets
A **planet** is a large, round body that moves around the Sun in a fixed path called an **orbit**. Planets do **not** make their own light – they shine because they **reflect sunlight**.

There are **eight** planets. In order from the Sun, they are: **Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus** and **Neptune**.
A sentence to remember the order: "**M**y **V**ery **E**ager **M**other **J**ust **S**erved **U**s **N**oodles."

FIG: c6-sci-12-solar-system.svg | Fig. 12.2: The eight planets of the solar system, in order from the Sun (sizes and distances are not to scale)

| Planet | Some facts |
| Mercury | Nearest to the Sun; smallest planet; no moon |
| Venus | Hottest and brightest planet in our sky; often called the morning or evening "star"; no moon |
| Earth | The only planet known to have life; has liquid water and air; one moon |
| Mars | Called the **red planet** because of its reddish soil; two small moons |
| Jupiter | The **largest** planet; a giant made mostly of gases; many moons |
| Saturn | Famous for its beautiful **rings** made of ice and rock; many moons |
| Uranus | An icy giant that spins tipped on its side |
| Neptune | Farthest from the Sun; very cold and windy |

- The four inner planets – Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars – are small and **rocky**.
- The four outer planets – Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune – are **giant** planets, made mostly of gases and ice.
- **Pluto**, once called the ninth planet, is now called a **dwarf planet**.

> **Remember:** Stars give out their own light. Planets only reflect light from the Sun.

## 12.5 Other Members of the Solar System
### Moons
A body that moves around a planet is called its **satellite**. The **Moon** is the Earth's natural satellite. It has no light of its own – it reflects sunlight. The Moon has no air or water, and its surface has many **craters** (bowl-shaped pits) made by rocks crashing into it.
The Moon seems to change its shape every night, from a thin crescent to a full Moon and back. These shapes are called the **phases of the Moon**. They happen because we see different amounts of the Moon's sunlit half as it moves around the Earth.

FIG: c6-sci-12-moon-phases.svg | Fig. 12.3: Some phases of the Moon, from new moon to full moon

### Asteroids
**Asteroids** are rocky bodies, much smaller than planets, that move around the Sun. Most of them are found in a wide band between Mars and Jupiter, called the **asteroid belt**.

### Comets
**Comets** are made of ice, dust and rock. They move around the Sun in long, stretched orbits. When a comet comes near the Sun, its ice turns into gas and it develops a long, glowing **tail** that always points **away from the Sun**. **Halley's Comet** returns to our sky about every 76 years.

### Meteors and meteorites
Small pieces of rock moving in space are called **meteoroids**. When one enters the Earth's atmosphere at great speed, it gets so hot that it burns up, making a bright streak of light. This is a **meteor**, often called a "shooting star". If a piece survives and reaches the ground, it is called a **meteorite**. The **Lonar lake** in Maharashtra was formed when a meteorite struck the Earth thousands of years ago.

## 12.6 Artificial Satellites and India in Space
Human-made objects that go around the Earth are called **artificial satellites**. They are used for weather forecasting, TV and phone communication, navigation (maps) and studying the Earth and space.

India's space agency, the **Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO)**, has achieved a great deal:
- **Aryabhata** (1975) – India's first satellite, named after the great astronomer.
- **Chandrayaan-1** (2008) – India's first mission to the Moon; it helped discover signs of water on the Moon.
- **Mangalyaan** (Mars Orbiter Mission, 2013–14) – reached Mars on India's very first attempt.
- **Chandrayaan-3** (2023) – landed near the Moon's **south pole**, making India the first country to land there. **23 August** is celebrated as **National Space Day**.
- **Aditya-L1** (2023) – India's first mission to study the Sun.

> **Did you know?**
> **Rakesh Sharma** became the first Indian to travel into space in 1984. When asked how India looked from space, he replied, "**Saare jahan se achha**" – better than the whole world!

## 12.7 Light Pollution
In big cities, it is hard to see many stars at night. Bright street lights, signboards and buildings light up the sky so much that the faint light of stars is hidden. This is called **light pollution**. It also confuses birds that fly at night and insects, and wastes electricity.
We can reduce it by switching off unnecessary lights, pointing outdoor lights downwards and using covered lamps.
