## Tenses – advanced use
- **Present perfect vs simple past:** "I **have visited** Ladakh" (experience, time not stated) vs "I **visited** Ladakh in 2023" (time stated).
- **Past perfect continuous:** an action going on before another past action – "She **had been studying** for three hours when the power went out."
- **Future perfect:** completed before a future time – "By June, they **will have built** the bridge."
- **Future continuous:** in progress at a future time – "This time tomorrow, I **will be flying** to Chennai."

## Modals – deeper meanings
- **Ability:** can, could, be able to – "She **will be able to** swim soon."
- **Permission:** may, can, could – "**May** I leave early?"
- **Obligation:** must (speaker's view), have to (external rule) – "I **must** call Mum." "We **have to** wear uniforms."
- **Deduction:** must (sure yes), can't (sure no), might/may (possible) – "He **must** be at home; the lights are on." "That **can't** be true."
- **Advice:** should, ought to, had better – "You **had better** leave now."
- **Past possibility/regret:** should have, could have, might have + V3 – "You **should have told** me."

## Semi-modals
need, dare, used to – "You **needn't** worry." "He **used to** live in Pune."
> 💡 "Must not" = prohibited; "don't have to" = not necessary.

## Let's check
Q: Fill in: "By next year, she ___ (complete) her course."
A: will have completed.
Q: Which modal shows logical certainty that something is NOT true?
A: can't.
Q: Correct: "You must to submit the form."
A: You must submit the form.

## Voice – all forms
**Active → passive:** object becomes subject + be (in tense) + V3 + (by agent).
- Simple present: They **speak** English. → English **is spoken**.
- Present continuous: She **is cleaning** the room. → The room **is being cleaned**.
- Present perfect: He **has repaired** the bike. → The bike **has been repaired**.
- Simple past: Edison **invented** the bulb. → The bulb **was invented** by Edison.
- Past continuous: They **were building** a dam. → A dam **was being built**.
- Past perfect: She **had posted** the letter. → The letter **had been posted**.
- Future: They **will open** the mall. → The mall **will be opened**.
- Modal: We **should save** water. → Water **should be saved**.

## Passive in formal writing
Used in reports, science and news, where the action matters more than the doer:
"The solution **was heated** for five minutes." "Three people **were injured** in the accident."

## Impersonal passive
"People say that he is rich." → "**It is said** that he is rich." / "He **is said to be** rich."
> 💡 Continuous tenses with "being" exist only in present and past in passive; "will be being" isn't used.

## Let's check
Q: Change to passive: "The police arrested the thief."
A: The thief was arrested by the police.
Q: Change to passive: "We must follow the rules."
A: The rules must be followed.
Q: Rewrite: "People believe that the house is haunted." (It is…)
A: It is believed that the house is haunted.

## Reported speech – complete guide
**Reporting verb in the past → backshift** tenses, change pronouns and time/place words.
- "I **am** working **now**," he said. → He said that he **was** working **then**.
- "We **went** there **yesterday**," they said. → They said that they **had gone** there **the previous day**.
- "I **will** finish it **tomorrow**," she said. → She said that she **would** finish it **the next day**.

## Questions
- "Have you seen my keys?" Dad asked. → Dad asked me **whether I had seen** his keys.
- "Why are you crying?" the teacher asked. → The teacher asked **why I was crying**.

## Commands, requests, advice
- "Switch off the lights," Mother said. → Mother **told** us **to switch off** the lights.
- "You should rest," the doctor said. → The doctor **advised** me **to rest**.
- "Let's go for a walk," he said. → He **suggested going** for a walk / **suggested that we should go** for a walk.

## Exclamations and wishes
- "Hurrah! We've won!" they said. → They **exclaimed with joy** that they had won.
- "May you live long!" Grandma said. → Grandma **blessed** me **that I might live long**.

## No backshift when…
- the reporting verb is present: She **says**, "I'm tired." → She says that she **is** tired.
- the statement is a universal truth or still true.
> 💡 Choose a precise reporting verb: advised, warned, begged, suggested, promised, complained.

## Let's check
Q: Report: "I can't find my phone," said Priya.
A: Priya said that she couldn't find her phone.
Q: Report: "Did you call me?" he asked.
A: He asked whether/if I had called him.
Q: Report: "Don't go near the fire," Mother said to the child.
A: Mother warned the child not to go near the fire.

## Types of sentences by structure
- **Simple:** one independent clause – "The rain stopped."
- **Compound:** two or more independent clauses joined by and, but, or, so, yet – "The rain stopped, **and** the children ran out."
- **Complex:** one independent + one or more dependent clauses – "**When the rain stopped**, the children ran out."
- **Compound-complex:** two independent + at least one dependent – "When the rain stopped, the children ran out, but their parents stayed inside."

## Dependent clauses
- **Noun clause:** "I wonder **whether he will come**."
- **Relative (adjective) clause:** "The book **that you lent me** was exciting."
- **Adverb clause:** time (when, after), reason (because, since), condition (if, unless), contrast (although, though), purpose (so that).

## Conditionals
- **Zero:** facts – If you heat ice, it **melts**.
- **First:** real future – If it rains, we **will stay** in.
- **Second:** unreal present – If I **were** a bird, I **would fly**.
- **Third:** unreal past – If you **had studied**, you **would have passed**.

## Transforming sentences
"He is poor. He is honest." → Simple: "**Despite being** poor, he is honest." Compound: "He is poor **but** honest." Complex: "**Although** he is poor, he is honest."
> 💡 Varying sentence types makes writing more interesting.

## Let's check
Q: Identify the type: "Because she was ill, she missed the exam."
A: Complex sentence.
Q: Complete the second conditional: "If I had wings, I ___."
A: would fly.
Q: Combine into a compound sentence: "The bell rang. The students left."
A: The bell rang, and the students left.

## Critical reading
Beyond finding answers, good readers think about **purpose, tone, bias and evidence**.
- **Main idea:** what is the passage mostly about?
- **Tone:** the writer's attitude – serious, humorous, critical, hopeful.
- **Fact vs opinion:** facts can be checked; opinions are beliefs.
- **Inference:** reading between the lines.

## Read the passage: Plastic in Our Oceans
Every year, more than eight million tonnes of plastic enter the oceans. Much of it comes from rivers carrying waste from cities. Sea turtles mistake plastic bags for jellyfish; seabirds feed bottle caps to their chicks. Tiny pieces called **microplastics** have been found in fish, salt and even drinking water.
Some argue that recycling alone will solve the problem. However, only a small fraction of plastic ever made has been recycled. Experts agree that we must reduce single-use plastic, design products that last, and hold companies responsible for the waste they create. Change, they say, must begin both in parliaments and in our own kitchens.

## Notice the writer's craft
- **Statistics** ("eight million tonnes") give evidence.
- **Vivid examples** (turtles, seabirds) create emotion.
- The final sentence links big policy to personal action.
> 💡 Words like "however" signal a contrast – the writer's real view often follows.

## Let's check
Q: What are microplastics?
A: Tiny pieces of plastic found in fish, salt and water.
Q: Why does the writer say recycling alone is not enough?
A: Because only a small fraction of plastic has ever been recycled.
Q: Is "Change must begin in our kitchens" a fact or an opinion?
A: An opinion.

## Formal letter types
- **Letter of complaint** – to an authority or company about a problem.
- **Letter of enquiry** – asking for information (courses, products).
- **Letter to the editor** – raising public issues.
- **Application** – for a job, leave or permission.

## Sample: letter of complaint
**Subject: Complaint about a defective water purifier**
Sir/Madam,
I purchased a water purifier (Model AquaPure X2, invoice no. 45821) from your Indore store on 2 March 2026. Within two weeks, the filter began leaking and the purifier stopped working. Despite two calls to your customer care, no technician has visited.
I request you to replace the product or refund the amount at the earliest. Copies of the invoice and warranty card are enclosed.
Yours faithfully,
Rohan Kulkarni

## Emails
- **To / Cc / Subject** fields.
- Greeting: Dear Mr Sharma, / Dear Sir,
- Short paragraphs; clear request; polite close: Regards / Best regards, name.
- Attach documents if needed and mention them.
> 💡 Be specific: give dates, numbers and exactly what action you want.

## Let's check
Q: What should a complaint letter clearly state?
A: The problem, details (dates, numbers) and the action you want.
Q: What does "Cc" in an email mean?
A: Carbon copy – sending a copy to other people.
Q: Which closing goes with "Dear Sir/Madam"?
A: Yours faithfully.

## Essay writing
- **Introduction:** hook (a question, quote or striking fact) + topic statement.
- **Body:** 3 paragraphs, each with a topic sentence, explanation and example.
- **Conclusion:** summarise and end with a thought or call to action.
**Types:** descriptive, narrative, argumentative (for/against), reflective.

## Sample argumentative outline: Should mobile phones be allowed in schools?
- Intro: Phones are everywhere; schools must decide.
- For: emergencies, learning apps, research.
- Against: distraction, cyberbullying, cheating, less outdoor play.
- Conclusion: allow limited, supervised use.

## Article writing
Written for a newspaper or magazine.
- **Title** (catchy) and **by-line** (By Aisha Khan).
- Introduction, body with facts and examples, conclusion with suggestions.
- Topics: "The Joy of Reading", "Road Safety Begins with Us", "Why Sports Matter".

## Language tips
Use linking words: firstly, moreover, however, therefore, in conclusion. Vary sentence length. Avoid repetition.
> 💡 An argumentative essay is stronger when you mention and answer the other side's view.

## Let's check
Q: What is a by-line?
A: The writer's name below the title of an article.
Q: Give two ways to start an essay with a hook.
A: A question, a quotation or a surprising fact.
Q: What should an argumentative essay's conclusion do?
A: Summarise the arguments and give a clear opinion or suggestion.

## Prose: The Gift of the Magi (O. Henry, summary)
Della and Jim, a poor young couple, want to buy each other Christmas gifts. Della has only $1.87. She sells her beautiful long hair to buy a platinum chain for Jim's prized gold watch. Jim, meanwhile, sells his watch to buy a set of expensive combs for Della's hair. When they exchange gifts, both are useless – yet they realise their love is the greatest treasure.
**Theme:** true love means selfless sacrifice. **Device:** **situational irony** – the outcome is the opposite of what was expected.

## Poem: If— (lines by Rudyard Kipling)
If you can keep your head when all about you
Are losing theirs and blaming it on you,
If you can trust yourself when all men doubt you,
But make allowance for their doubting too…
Yours is the Earth and everything that's in it,
And—which is more—you'll be a Man, my son!

## Understanding the poem
A father advises his son on how to become a mature person: stay calm in crises, believe in yourself while respecting others, handle success and failure equally. The poem uses **repetition** ("If you can…") to build up to its conclusion.
> 💡 Irony, symbolism and theme are key ideas when studying literature.

## Let's check
Q: What did Della sell to buy Jim's gift?
A: Her long hair.
Q: What kind of irony is used in "The Gift of the Magi"?
A: Situational irony.
Q: What does "keep your head" mean in the poem?
A: Stay calm in difficult situations.
