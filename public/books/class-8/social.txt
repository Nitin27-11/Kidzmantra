## Dates and periods
History is not just about dates, but dates help us place events in order. **James Mill**, a British writer, divided Indian history into **Hindu, Muslim and British** periods – a view now rejected because it ignores the variety of each period. Historians today use **ancient, medieval and modern**.
Some argue the "modern" period under British rule was also a time of **colonialism** – when one country conquers and rules another, bringing political, economic, social and cultural changes.

## Sources for modern history
- **Official records:** the British kept careful records of every instruction, plan and policy. They set up **archives** and the **National Archives of India** (Delhi) and record rooms.
- **Surveys:** of land (revenue surveys), botany, zoology, archaeology, anthropology, forests. The **Census** every 10 years since 1881.
- **Unofficial sources:** diaries, autobiographies, popular books, newspapers, letters.

## Whose history?
Official records show the rulers' view. To understand ordinary people – peasants, tribals, workers, women – historians use other sources too.
> 💡 Before printing, clerks called calligraphists copied official documents by hand.

A: Since 1881.

## Trade to rule
- **1600:** the **East India Company** got a charter from Queen Elizabeth I to trade with the East.
- The Portuguese (Vasco da Gama, 1498), Dutch and French also came. They competed fiercely for **cotton, silk, pepper, cloves, cardamom and cinnamon**.
- The Company set up **factories** (trading posts) at Surat, Madras (Fort St George), Calcutta (Fort William).

## Battle of Plassey (1757)
Nawab **Siraj-ud-Daulah** of Bengal opposed the Company's misuse of trade privileges. **Robert Clive** defeated him at **Plassey**, helped by the betrayal of **Mir Jafar**. This was the Company's first major victory.
**Battle of Buxar (1764):** the Company defeated the combined forces of Mir Qasim, the Nawab of Awadh and the Mughal emperor. In **1765**, the Company got the **Diwani** – the right to collect revenue in Bengal, Bihar and Orissa.

## Expanding control
- **Subsidiary Alliance** (Lord Wellesley): Indian rulers had to keep British troops and pay for them; they couldn't have their own army.
- **Tipu Sultan** of Mysore ("Tiger of Mysore") fought the British; killed in 1799 at Seringapatam.
- **Maratha wars** ended with Maratha defeat (1818).
- **Doctrine of Lapse** (Lord Dalhousie): if a ruler died without a male heir, his kingdom would pass to the Company – Satara, Jhansi, Nagpur were taken. **Awadh** was taken in 1856 claiming misrule.

## Administration
Three **Presidencies**: Bengal, Madras, Bombay, each under a Governor; the Governor-General at the top. Collectors collected revenue; new courts and a Company army (**sepoys**) were set up.
> 💡 Warren Hastings was the first Governor-General (1773).

## Revenue systems
The Company wanted steady revenue.
- **Permanent Settlement (1793, Bengal, Lord Cornwallis):** **zamindars** collected fixed revenue from peasants; the amount was fixed forever. Peasants suffered; many zamindars lost land when they couldn't pay.
- **Mahalwari system (North-West, Holt Mackenzie):** revenue fixed for a whole village (**mahal**) and revised periodically; collected by the village headman.
- **Ryotwari system (South India, Thomas Munro):** settled directly with cultivators (**ryots**). Revenue was often too high; peasants fell into debt.

## Commercial crops for Europe
The Company forced cultivation of crops needed in Britain:
- **Indigo** (blue dye) – grown under two systems: **nij** (planters' own land with hired labour) and **ryoti** (ryots forced to grow it under contracts, with loans). Indigo exhausted the soil and ruined farmers.
- Others: jute, tea, sugarcane, wheat, cotton, rice, opium (sold to China).

## Indigo revolt (1859)
Ryots in Bengal refused to grow indigo; they attacked indigo factories. The **Indigo Commission** (1860) said ryots couldn't be forced. In **1917**, **Mahatma Gandhi** went to **Champaran** (Bihar) to support indigo farmers – his first satyagraha in India.
> 💡 "Neel Darpan", a play by Dinabandhu Mitra, showed the cruelty of indigo planters.

## Let's check

Q: What was the ryotwari system?
A: Revenue was settled directly with the cultivators (ryots).
Q: Where did Gandhi lead a movement for indigo farmers?
A: Champaran, Bihar (1917).

## Tribal life
Tribal groups lived in forests and hills with their own customs. They practised:
- **Jhum (shifting) cultivation** – clearing small patches by burning, farming them, then moving on.
- Hunting and gathering – Khonds of Orissa, Baigas.
- Herding – Van Gujjars, Gaddis, Bakarwals, Labadis.
- Settled farming – Mundas of Chotanagpur.

## How colonial rule affected tribals
- **Chiefs** lost power and had to pay tribute to the British.
- The British wanted **settled farming** and taxed land.
- **Forest laws** declared forests state property – **reserved forests** where tribals couldn't cut wood, graze cattle or farm.
- **Traders and moneylenders** (dikus – outsiders) exploited tribals.
- Many had to work in **tea plantations** and **mines** far from home, for low wages.

## Birsa Munda (1875–1900)
A Munda leader from **Chotanagpur** (Jharkhand). He called on Mundas to recover their glory, give up bad practices, and drive out missionaries, moneylenders, landlords and the British (**dikus**). He promised a **golden age (satyug)**. Arrested in 1895; after release, led attacks on police stations and churches. He died of cholera in jail in 1900, aged 25.
**Result:** the **Chotanagpur Tenancy Act (1908)** – tribal land couldn't easily be taken by outsiders.
> 💡 15 November, Birsa Munda's birth anniversary, is celebrated as Janjatiya Gaurav Divas.

A: Outsiders – moneylenders, traders, landlords, missionaries and the British.

## Causes of the revolt
- **Political:** annexation by the Doctrine of Lapse and of Awadh; the Mughal emperor's position was insulted.
- **Economic:** high taxes, peasants losing land, artisans ruined by British goods.
- **Social and religious:** laws against sati, widow remarriage allowed, missionaries' activities – people feared their religion was in danger.
- **Military:** sepoys had low pay, faced discrimination, and were sent overseas (crossing the sea was against some beliefs).
- **Immediate cause:** new **Enfield rifle** cartridges, said to be greased with cow and pig fat; soldiers had to bite them.

## The revolt
- **29 March 1857:** **Mangal Pandey** attacked his officers at **Barrackpore**; he was hanged.
- **10 May 1857:** sepoys at **Meerut** rebelled, marched to **Delhi** and declared **Bahadur Shah Zafar** their leader.
- The revolt spread to Kanpur (**Nana Saheb**, **Tantia Tope**), Lucknow (**Begum Hazrat Mahal**), Jhansi (**Rani Lakshmibai**), Bihar (**Kunwar Singh**), and Faizabad (**Maulvi Ahmadullah Shah**).

## Suppression
The British recaptured Delhi (September 1857). Bahadur Shah Zafar was exiled to **Rangoon**; his sons were killed. Rani Lakshmibai died fighting in June 1858. Tantia Tope was captured and executed in 1859.

## Results
- **1858:** the Crown took over from the Company. The **Viceroy** represented the British monarch.
- Rulers were assured their kingdoms wouldn't be annexed.
- The army was reorganised; more soldiers from Gurkhas, Sikhs and Pathans.
- The British respected religious customs and protected landlords.
> 💡 V.D. Savarkar called it the "First War of Indian Independence".

## Rise of nationalism
In the late 19th century, Indians began to see themselves as one nation and demanded rights.
- **1885:** the **Indian National Congress** was founded in Bombay; **A.O. Hume** helped; leaders like **Dadabhai Naoroji** ("drain of wealth" theory), **Pherozeshah Mehta**, **Gopal Krishna Gokhale** (the **Moderates** – petitions and dialogue).
- **Radicals (Extremists):** **Bal Gangadhar Tilak** ("Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it!"), **Lala Lajpat Rai**, **Bipin Chandra Pal** – "Lal-Bal-Pal".
- **1905: Partition of Bengal** by Lord Curzon → **Swadeshi movement**: boycott of British goods, use of Indian goods.
- **1906:** All India Muslim League formed.

## The Gandhian era
- **Mahatma Gandhi** returned from South Africa in **1915**; led **Champaran** (1917), **Kheda** and **Ahmedabad** (1918) movements.
- **1919:** **Rowlatt Act** (detention without trial) → protests; **Jallianwala Bagh massacre** (13 April 1919, Amritsar), General Dyer fired on a peaceful crowd.
- **1920–22: Non-Cooperation Movement** and **Khilafat Movement** – boycott of schools, courts, foreign cloth. Called off after **Chauri Chaura** violence (1922).
- **1929:** Lahore Congress, Jawaharlal Nehru presiding, demanded **Purna Swaraj** (complete independence); **26 January 1930** celebrated as Independence Day.
- **1930: Dandi March / Salt Satyagraha** – Gandhi walked 240 miles to Dandi to make salt, beginning **Civil Disobedience**.
- **1942: Quit India Movement** – "Do or Die".

## Other paths
**Bhagat Singh**, **Chandrashekhar Azad**, **Sukhdev**, **Rajguru** – revolutionaries. **Subhas Chandra Bose** formed the **Indian National Army (INA)**: "Give me blood, and I will give you freedom!"

## Independence and Partition
**15 August 1947:** India became independent; the country was partitioned into India and Pakistan, with terrible violence and the migration of millions.
> 💡 Many women like Sarojini Naidu, Kasturba Gandhi, Aruna Asaf Ali and Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay played leading roles.

## A new nation
At independence (1947), India faced huge challenges: refugees from Partition, about **500 princely states** to unite, poverty, illiteracy and food shortages.

## Integrating princely states
**Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel** (the Iron Man of India), with V.P. Menon, persuaded most princely states to join India. Hyderabad, Junagadh and Kashmir took more effort.

## The Constitution
- Drafted by the **Constituent Assembly**; **Dr B.R. Ambedkar** chaired the Drafting Committee.
- Adopted on **26 November 1949**; came into force on **26 January 1950** (Republic Day).
- Gave **universal adult franchise**, equality before the law, abolition of untouchability, reservation for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.
- Divided powers between the Centre and states (Union, State and Concurrent lists).

## States reorganised by language
After protests, especially **Potti Sriramulu's** fast unto death for a Telugu-speaking state (Andhra, 1952), states were reorganised on **linguistic lines** (1956).

## Planning for development
The **Planning Commission** (1950) made **Five-Year Plans**. Focus on heavy industries (steel plants at Bhilai, Rourkela), dams (**Bhakra Nangal** – called "temples of modern India" by Nehru), agriculture (**Green Revolution** in the 1960s).

## Foreign policy
**Non-alignment** – not joining either the USA or the USSR bloc during the Cold War. Nehru, Nasser (Egypt) and Tito (Yugoslavia) led the Non-Aligned Movement.
> 💡 The first general elections (1951–52) were the largest democratic exercise in the world at the time.

## What is a resource?
Anything that can be used to satisfy a need and has **utility (usability)** and **value**. Time and technology make things into resources – water became a resource for electricity when we learnt to use it.

## Types of resources
- **Natural:** from nature – air, water, soil, minerals, forests.
  - **Renewable:** replenished quickly – solar and wind energy, water, forests (if used wisely).
  - **Non-renewable:** limited – coal, petroleum, natural gas.
  - **Ubiquitous** (found everywhere – air) and **localised** (in certain places – copper, iron ore).
  - **Actual** (quantity known, being used – Ruhr coal) and **potential** (not yet used fully – uranium in Ladakh).
- **Human-made:** created by people – buildings, roads, machines, bridges, vehicles, technology.
- **Human resources:** people with their skills, knowledge and health. **Human resource development** = improving skills through education and health.

## Conserving resources
Using resources carefully so they last for future generations. **Sustainable development** means meeting today's needs without harming the future.
**Principles:** respect all life, improve quality of life, conserve the Earth's variety, minimise depletion, change attitudes, let communities care for their environment.
> 💡 Reduce, reuse and recycle – everyone can help conserve resources.

## Let's check
Q: What gives a thing value as a resource?
A: Its usefulness (utility) and value, which depend on time and technology.
Q: Give an example of a ubiquitous resource.
A: Air.
Q: What is sustainable development?
A: Development that meets present needs without harming the ability of future generations to meet theirs.

## Land
Only about **30%** of Earth's surface is land, and not all of it is habitable.
**Land use:** agriculture, forests, mining, roads, housing, industry. Land is owned privately or as community land.
**Threats:** growing population leads to land degradation, landslides, soil erosion and desertification.
**Landslides:** mass movement of rock, soil and debris down slopes. Prevention: retaining walls, planting trees, surface drainage, hazard mapping.

## Soil
Formed by **weathering** of rocks, influenced by parent rock, climate, relief, flora and fauna, and time.
**Soil degradation:** deforestation, overgrazing, chemical fertilisers, rain wash, landslides, floods.
**Conservation methods:**
- **Mulching** – covering bare soil with organic matter.
- **Contour barriers** and **contour ploughing** – along the contours of hills.
- **Rock dams**, **terrace farming** (steps on slopes).
- **Intercropping**, **shelter belts** (rows of trees to block wind).

## Water
Only about **2.7%** of water is fresh. **Scarcity** due to population, overuse, pollution. Conserve with rainwater harvesting, drip/sprinkler irrigation, protecting forests, and canals lined to reduce seepage.

## Natural vegetation and wildlife
Forests provide timber, food and habitats and maintain the ecological balance. Threats: deforestation, poaching, forest fires. Conservation: **national parks**, **wildlife sanctuaries**, **biosphere reserves**; **CITES** (international agreement) controls trade in endangered species.
> 💡 Van Mahotsav is a tree-planting week celebrated in July.

## Let's check
Q: What is mulching?
A: Covering bare soil with organic matter to keep moisture and prevent erosion.
Q: How does terrace farming help?
A: It reduces soil erosion on slopes by making flat steps.
Q: What is CITES?
A: An international agreement that controls trade in endangered plants and animals.

## Agriculture
Growing crops, fruits and vegetables, raising livestock. It is a **primary activity**. About half of India's workers depend on farming.
**Economic activities:** primary (farming, fishing, mining), **secondary** (manufacturing), **tertiary** (services – transport, banking, trade).

## Farm system
**Inputs** (seeds, fertilisers, machinery, labour) → **operations** (ploughing, sowing, irrigation, weeding, harvesting) → **outputs** (crops, wool, dairy, poultry).

## Types of farming
- **Subsistence farming:** to meet the family's needs; small land, simple tools.
  - **Intensive:** small plot, much labour, high output (rice in monsoon Asia).
  - **Primitive:** **shifting cultivation** (jhumming in the North-East) and **nomadic herding**.
- **Commercial farming:** for sale; large areas, machines.
  - **Plantations:** one crop – tea (Assam), coffee (Karnataka), rubber, sugarcane, bananas.

## Major crops
- **Rice:** needs high temperature, high humidity and rainfall; alluvial clayey soil. India and China are leading producers.
- **Wheat:** moderate temperature, rainfall; well-drained loamy soil. Grown in winter in India.
- **Millets** (jowar, bajra, ragi): hardy; grow in poor soil.
- **Cotton:** high temperature, light rainfall, black soil.
- **Jute:** "golden fibre"; high temperature, heavy rainfall, alluvial soil (West Bengal).
- **Tea and coffee:** hill slopes, well-drained soil.
> 💡 The Green Revolution of the 1960s used high-yielding seeds, fertilisers and irrigation to boost wheat and rice production.

## What is industry?
**Manufacturing** – changing raw materials into more useful products. It's a **secondary activity**.

## Classifying industries
- **Raw materials:** agro-based (cotton, sugar), mineral-based (iron and steel), marine-based (seafood), forest-based (paper, furniture).
- **Size:** small-scale (handloom, pottery) and large-scale (cars, steel).
- **Ownership:** private, public (government – BHEL, SAIL), joint, cooperative (Amul dairy).

## Factors affecting location
Raw materials, land, water, labour, power, capital, transport, markets.

## Industrial system and regions
**Inputs → processes → outputs.**
Major industrial regions: Mumbai–Pune, Bengaluru–Tamil Nadu, Hugli (Kolkata), Ahmedabad–Vadodara, Chotanagpur.

## Important industries
- **Iron and steel:** needs iron ore, coal, limestone and manganese. **TISCO, Jamshedpur** (1907) – near raw materials, rivers (Subarnarekha), rail and markets.
- **Cotton textiles:** **Mumbai** and **Ahmedabad** ("Manchester of India") – humid climate, port, cotton-growing areas. Handloom and powerloom are important too.
- **Information Technology:** **Bengaluru** ("Silicon Valley of India") – educated workers, good infrastructure, pleasant climate. Also Hyderabad, Pune, Chennai, Gurugram.
> 💡 An industrial disaster: the Bhopal gas tragedy (1984), when toxic gas leaked from a pesticide factory.

Q: Which city is called the Silicon Valley of India?
A: Bengaluru.
Q: Give an example of a cooperative industry.
A: Amul dairy.

## People as a resource
People are a nation's greatest resource when they are educated, healthy and skilled. Their abilities create other resources.

## Population distribution
The way people are spread across the Earth. More than **90%** of the world's people live on about **30%** of the land.
- **Densely populated:** fertile plains, river valleys, cities – South and East Asia, Europe.
- **Sparsely populated:** deserts, high mountains, polar regions, rainforests.

## Population density
**Density = number of people ÷ area** (people per sq km). India's density is about **382** (Census 2011).

## Factors affecting distribution
- **Geographical:** topography, climate, soil, water, minerals.
- **Social, cultural and economic:** jobs, religious and cultural places, industries, cities.

## Population change
- **Birth rate:** live births per 1,000 people.
- **Death rate:** deaths per 1,000 people.
- **Migration:** movement of people – **emigrants** leave a country; **immigrants** come in. Rural-to-urban migration is common.
- Natural growth = birth rate – death rate.

## Population composition
Structure by **age, sex, literacy, health, occupation and income**. Shown by a **population pyramid**. The **sex ratio** = number of females per 1,000 males.
> 💡 A young population with good education and health can greatly help a country grow.

## Let's check
Q: What is population density?
A: The number of people living per square kilometre.
Q: Who is an immigrant?
A: A person who comes into a country to live.
Q: What does a population pyramid show?
A: The age and sex structure of a population.

## Why do we need a constitution?
A **Constitution** is a written document that lays down the rules by which a country is governed.
- It sets out the **ideals** the country believes in.
- It defines the **form of government** and the powers of each part.
- It **limits** the powers of the government so it can't misuse them.
- It **protects minorities** from the majority.
- It saves us from ourselves – stops decisions made in anger or haste.

## Key features of the Indian Constitution
1. **Federalism:** more than one level of government – central, state, and panchayati raj.
2. **Parliamentary form of government:** representatives are elected by the people (universal adult franchise).
3. **Separation of powers:** **legislature** (makes laws), **executive** (implements), **judiciary** (interprets and settles disputes) – each checks the others.
4. **Fundamental Rights:** the "conscience" of the Constitution.
5. **Secularism:** the state does not promote any religion.

## Fundamental Rights
1. Right to **Equality**
2. Right to **Freedom** (speech, expression, movement, profession)
3. Right against **Exploitation** (no forced labour, child labour, trafficking)
4. Right to **Freedom of Religion**
5. **Cultural and Educational** Rights (minorities)
6. Right to **Constitutional Remedies** – go to court if rights are violated. Dr Ambedkar called it the "heart and soul" of the Constitution.

## Fundamental Duties and Directive Principles
- **Fundamental Duties:** respect the flag and anthem, protect the environment, promote harmony, etc.
- **Directive Principles:** guidelines for the government to work for social and economic welfare.
> 💡 The Right to Education (2009) made education free and compulsory for children aged 6–14.

## Let's check
Q: Name the three organs of government.
A: Legislature, executive and judiciary.
Q: Which right did Dr Ambedkar call the heart and soul of the Constitution?
A: The Right to Constitutional Remedies.
Q: What is federalism?
A: Having more than one level of government, such as central and state.

## What is secularism?
**Secularism** means the **separation of religion from the State**. In India:
- No religious group dominates another.
- No members of one religion dominate others of the same religion.
- The **State does not enforce any religion** nor take away people's religious freedom.

## Why is it important?
History shows that when a state favours one religion, others face discrimination and persecution – e.g. **Hitler's Germany** persecuted Jews. Secularism protects everyone's freedom and equality.

## How the Indian State maintains secularism
1. **Distance:** the government doesn't promote any religion. Government schools can't hold religious prayers or celebrate one religion's festivals officially.
2. **Non-interference:** respects religious practices – e.g. Sikhs may wear turbans instead of helmets on two-wheelers.
3. **Intervention:** may step in to end harmful practices – e.g. **untouchability** was abolished.

## Indian vs American secularism
In the **USA**, there's strict separation: the State doesn't interfere in religion at all. In **India**, the State can intervene in religious matters to ensure equality and justice.

## Challenges
Discrimination still happens. The Constitution, courts and citizens must work to protect secular values.
> 💡 The word "secular" was added to the Preamble by the 42nd Amendment (1976), but secular principles were always part of the Constitution.

## Let's check
Q: What does secularism mean?
A: The separation of religion from the State, with equal respect and freedom for all religions.
Q: Give an example of the State's non-interference in religion.
A: Sikhs are allowed to wear turbans instead of helmets.
Q: How does Indian secularism differ from American secularism?
A: The Indian State can intervene in religion to ensure equality; the US keeps strict separation.

## Parliament
India's highest law-making body. It has the **President** and two houses:
- **Lok Sabha (House of the People):** directly elected by the people; up to **543 elected members**; term of 5 years. Members are **MPs**.
- **Rajya Sabha (Council of States):** members elected by state legislative assemblies; maximum **250** (12 nominated by the President); a permanent house – one-third retire every two years.

## Role of Parliament
- **Select the government:** the party or coalition with a majority in the Lok Sabha forms the government; its leader becomes the **Prime Minister**, who chooses the **Council of Ministers**.
- **Control and guide the government:** **Question Hour**, debates, motions; the opposition questions government actions.
- **Make laws** and approve the **budget**.

## How a law is made
1. A **Bill** (proposed law) is introduced in either house.
2. It is discussed and passed by both houses.
3. The **President** gives assent → it becomes an **Act** (law).
Example: the **Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005** came after long campaigns by women's groups.

## Unpopular laws
People can protest, write to MPs, approach courts. Courts can strike down laws that violate the Constitution.
> 💡 The Speaker presides over the Lok Sabha; the Vice-President chairs the Rajya Sabha.

## Let's check
Q: What are the two houses of Parliament?
A: Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha.
Q: Who becomes the Prime Minister?
A: The leader of the party or coalition with a majority in the Lok Sabha.
Q: When does a Bill become an Act?
A: After both houses pass it and the President gives assent.

## The judiciary
Courts that settle disputes and uphold the law.

## Roles
- **Dispute resolution:** between citizens, citizens and government, states, and the Centre and states.
- **Judicial review:** striking down laws that go against the Constitution.
- **Upholding the law and enforcing Fundamental Rights.**

## Independent judiciary
Judges should be free from the influence of the legislature, executive or the rich and powerful. In India, judges are appointed with little interference from other branches and are hard to remove.

## Structure of courts
1. **Supreme Court** (New Delhi) – the highest court; headed by the **Chief Justice of India**; established on 28 January 1950.
2. **High Courts** – in states.
3. **District Courts** – at district level, also called subordinate courts.
This is an **integrated system**: decisions of higher courts bind lower courts. People can **appeal** to a higher court.

## Branches of law
- **Criminal law:** offences like theft, murder, dowry harassment; starts with an **FIR** at a police station; punishment if guilty.
- **Civil law:** harm to rights – disputes over land, rent, divorce; the court gives relief such as compensation.

## Access to justice
Courts are open to all, but costs and delays are problems. **Public Interest Litigation (PIL)**: anyone can approach the High Court or Supreme Court on behalf of those whose rights are violated, even by writing a letter. **Free legal aid** helps the poor.
> 💡 "Justice delayed is justice denied" – speedy trials are a right.

## Let's check
Q: What is judicial review?
A: The power of courts to strike down laws that violate the Constitution.
Q: What is an FIR?
A: A First Information Report filed with the police to begin a criminal case.
Q: What is a PIL?
A: Public Interest Litigation – a case filed in public interest on behalf of those whose rights are violated.

## Public facilities
Facilities that everyone needs and that should be available to all: **water, healthcare, education, sanitation, electricity, public transport**.

## Water as a right
Water is essential for life. The **Right to Life** (Article 21) includes the right to safe drinking water, as courts have said. Yet many people – especially the poor – lack access and depend on unsafe sources or costly private tankers and bottled water.

## Role of the government
- Public facilities should be provided by the government because private companies work for profit and may not serve the poor.
- Money comes from **taxes** collected by the government; the **budget** decides spending.
- Example: **Chennai** – rich areas get more water than poor areas; tankers and borewells fill the gap.

## Good examples
- **Porto Alegre** (Brazil): almost everyone has safe water; the city involves people in budget decisions.
- In India, **Mumbai's** public water supply, **Hyderabad** and some towns have improved coverage.

## Sanitation
Many still lack toilets; the **Swachh Bharat Mission** aims to provide sanitation for all.

## Equality and public facilities
The Constitution's commitment to equality means public facilities must be available to **all**, rich and poor, in villages and cities.
> 💡 Public transport like buses and the metro is a public facility too – affordable and less polluting.

## Let's check
Q: Why should public facilities be provided by the government?
A: Because private companies work for profit and may not serve everyone, especially the poor.
Q: Which right includes the right to safe drinking water?
A: The Right to Life (Article 21).
Q: Name two public facilities.
A: Water, healthcare, education, sanitation, electricity, public transport.
