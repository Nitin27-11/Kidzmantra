## Integers review
Integers: …, –3, –2, –1, 0, 1, 2, 3, … They can be shown on a number line.

## Multiplying integers
- (+) × (+) = **+** → 4 × 3 = 12
- (–) × (–) = **+** → (–4) × (–3) = 12
- (+) × (–) = **–** → 4 × (–3) = –12
- An even number of negative factors gives a positive product; an odd number gives a negative product: (–1)³ = –1, (–1)⁴ = 1.

## Dividing integers
Same sign rules: (–20) ÷ (–4) = 5; 20 ÷ (–4) = –5. Division by 0 is not defined.

## Properties
- **Closure:** under +, –, × (not ÷).
- **Commutative and associative:** for + and × (not – or ÷).
- **Distributive:** a × (b + c) = ab + ac.
- **Identity:** 0 for addition, 1 for multiplication.

## Real-life example
A submarine is at –120 m and rises 45 m, then dives 30 m. Final position: –120 + 45 – 30 = **–105 m**.
> 💡 Temperature dropping 3°C every hour for 5 hours: change = 5 × (–3) = –15°C.

## Let's check
Q: (–6) × 7 = ?
A: –42.
Q: (–48) ÷ (–6) = ?
A: 8.
Q: (–2) × (–3) × (–5) = ?
A: –30.

## Multiplying fractions
Multiply numerators and denominators: ⅔ × ⁴⁄₅ = **⁸⁄₁₅**. Simplify before multiplying when possible: ³⁄₄ × ⁸⁄₉ = ²⁄₃.
"Of" means multiply: ¾ of 60 = 45.

## Dividing fractions
Multiply by the **reciprocal** (flip the second fraction): ⅗ ÷ ⁹⁄₁₀ = ⅗ × ¹⁰⁄₉ = **⅔**.
Reciprocal of ⁵⁄₇ is ⁷⁄₅. 0 has no reciprocal.

## Multiplying decimals
Multiply as whole numbers, then put as many decimal places as in both numbers together:
2.5 × 1.3 = 3.25 (25 × 13 = 325; 1 + 1 = 2 places).

## Dividing decimals
Make the divisor a whole number by moving both points the same number of places:
4.5 ÷ 0.15 = 450 ÷ 15 = **30**.
> 💡 Multiplying by a proper fraction makes a number smaller; dividing by it makes it bigger.

## Let's check
Q: ⅝ × ⁴⁄₁₅ = ?
A: ⅙.
Q: ¾ ÷ ⅜ = ?
A: 2.
Q: 0.6 × 0.07 = ?
A: 0.042.

## Measures of central tendency
A single value that represents a whole set of data.
- **Mean (average)** = sum of observations ÷ number of observations.
  Marks 40, 55, 60, 65, 80 → mean = 300 ÷ 5 = **60**.
- **Median** = the middle value when data is arranged in order. For 3, 7, 9, 12, 15 → **9**. If there is an even number of values, take the average of the two middle ones.
- **Mode** = the value that occurs most often. For 2, 4, 4, 5, 4, 7 → **4**.

## Range
Range = highest – lowest. For 12, 18, 25, 9 → 25 – 9 = **16**.

## Double bar graphs
Show two sets of data side by side, like marks in Maths and Science for each student.

## Chance and probability (introduction)
Some events are certain (the Sun rises), some impossible, some may or may not happen. Tossing a coin: two equally likely outcomes – head or tail.
> 💡 The mean can be affected by one very large or small value; the median isn't.

## Let's check
Q: Find the mean of 6, 8, 10, 12, 14.
A: 10.
Q: Find the median of 5, 1, 9, 3, 7.
A: 5.
Q: Find the mode of 3, 5, 5, 6, 3, 5.
A: 5.

## What is an equation?
An **equation** is a statement that two expressions are equal, with a variable: **3x + 4 = 19**. The value that makes it true is the **solution** (root).

## Solving equations (balance method)
Do the same thing to both sides to keep the balance.
3x + 4 = 19
3x = 19 – 4 = 15 (subtract 4 from both sides)
x = 15 ÷ 3 = **5** (divide both sides by 3)
**Check:** 3(5) + 4 = 19 ✔

## Transposing
Moving a term to the other side changes its operation: + becomes –, × becomes ÷.
x/4 – 2 = 3 → x/4 = 5 → x = **20**.

## Word problems
"Four times a number plus 7 is 35." → 4n + 7 = 35 → 4n = 28 → n = **7**.
"Raju's father is 4 years more than three times Raju's age. Father is 40." → 3r + 4 = 40 → r = **12**.
> 💡 Always check your answer by putting it back into the equation.

## Let's check
Q: Solve: 5y – 3 = 22.
A: y = 5.
Q: Solve: 2(x + 3) = 18.
A: x = 6.
Q: The sum of a number and 15 is 42. Find the number.
A: 27.

## Related angles
- **Complementary angles:** add to **90°** (30° and 60°).
- **Supplementary angles:** add to **180°** (110° and 70°).
- **Adjacent angles:** share a common arm and vertex.
- **Linear pair:** adjacent angles that are supplementary (form a straight line).
- **Vertically opposite angles:** formed when two lines cross; they are **equal**.

## Transversal and parallel lines
A **transversal** is a line that cuts two or more lines. If the lines are **parallel**:
- **Corresponding angles** are equal.
- **Alternate interior angles** are equal.
- **Interior angles on the same side** add to 180°.

## Example
Two lines cross; one angle is 65°. The vertically opposite angle is **65°**; each adjacent angle is 180° – 65° = **115°**.
> 💡 Railway tracks and the sleepers across them show parallel lines and a transversal.

## Let's check
Q: Find the complement of 35°.
A: 55°.
Q: Find the supplement of 128°.
A: 52°.
Q: If two parallel lines are cut by a transversal and one corresponding angle is 72°, what is the other?
A: 72°.

## Parts of a triangle
3 sides, 3 angles, 3 vertices. Special lines:
- **Median:** joins a vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side.
- **Altitude:** perpendicular from a vertex to the opposite side.

## Angle sum property
The three angles add to **180°**. If two angles are 50° and 60°, the third is **70°**.

## Exterior angle property
An exterior angle equals the **sum of the two interior opposite angles**. If interior opposite angles are 40° and 75°, the exterior angle = **115°**.

## Triangle inequality
The sum of any two sides is **greater** than the third. Can 3 cm, 4 cm and 8 cm form a triangle? 3 + 4 = 7 < 8 → **No**.

## Pythagoras theorem
In a **right-angled triangle**: (hypotenuse)² = (base)² + (perpendicular)².
Sides 3, 4 → hypotenuse = √(9 + 16) = √25 = **5**.
**Pythagorean triplets:** (3, 4, 5), (5, 12, 13), (8, 15, 17).
> 💡 The hypotenuse is always the longest side, opposite the right angle.

## Let's check
Q: Two angles of a triangle are 90° and 35°. Find the third.
A: 55°.
Q: Can 5 cm, 6 cm and 12 cm form a triangle?
A: No, because 5 + 6 = 11 < 12.
Q: Find the hypotenuse if the other sides are 6 cm and 8 cm.
A: 10 cm.

## Congruent figures
Figures that have **exactly the same shape and size** are **congruent** (≅). They fit exactly on top of each other. Example: two ₹10 coins.

## Congruence rules for triangles
Two triangles are congruent if:
- **SSS:** all three sides are equal.
- **SAS:** two sides and the **included** angle are equal.
- **ASA:** two angles and the **included** side are equal.
- **RHS:** in right triangles, the hypotenuse and one side are equal.

## Corresponding parts
If △ABC ≅ △PQR, then A ↔ P, B ↔ Q, C ↔ R, and AB = PQ, BC = QR, AC = PR. (CPCT: corresponding parts of congruent triangles are equal.)

## Not a rule
**AAA** does not prove congruence – triangles with the same angles can be different sizes (they are **similar**).
> 💡 The order of letters matters: △ABC ≅ △PQR matches A with P.

## Let's check
Q: Which rule: AB = PQ, BC = QR, ∠B = ∠Q?
A: SAS.
Q: Does AAA prove two triangles congruent?
A: No.
Q: What does RHS stand for?
A: Right angle–Hypotenuse–Side.

## Percentage review
- x% = x/100.
- Fraction to %: ³⁄₈ × 100 = 37.5%.
- % of a quantity: 15% of 480 = 72.
- Increase/decrease %: = (change ÷ original) × 100. Price rises from ₹200 to ₹250 → 25% increase.

## Profit and loss
- **Cost Price (CP):** price at which something is bought.
- **Selling Price (SP):** price at which it is sold.
- **Profit** = SP – CP (if SP > CP); **Loss** = CP – SP (if CP > SP).
- **Profit %** = (Profit ÷ CP) × 100; **Loss %** = (Loss ÷ CP) × 100.
Example: CP = ₹800, SP = ₹920 → profit ₹120 → profit % = 120/800 × 100 = **15%**.

## Simple interest
**SI = (P × R × T) ÷ 100**, where P = principal, R = rate % per year, T = time in years.
**Amount = P + SI.**
₹5,000 at 8% for 3 years → SI = 5,000 × 8 × 3 ÷ 100 = **₹1,200**; amount = ₹6,200.
> 💡 Profit and loss percentages are always calculated on the cost price.

## Let's check
Q: CP = ₹500, SP = ₹450. Find the loss %.
A: 10%.
Q: Find SI on ₹2,000 at 5% for 2 years.
A: ₹200.
Q: What is 40% of 350?
A: 140.

## What are rational numbers?
A **rational number** can be written as **p/q**, where p and q are integers and **q ≠ 0**. Examples: ³⁄₄, –⁵⁄₇, 6 (= ⁶⁄₁), 0, 0.5 (= ½).
- All integers and fractions are rational numbers.

## Standard form
Denominator positive, numerator and denominator have no common factor: –¹⁸⁄₄₈ = –⅜.

## Equivalent rational numbers
Multiply or divide both by the same non-zero number: ⅔ = –⁴⁄–₆ = ¹⁰⁄₁₅.

## Comparing
Make denominators the same: compare –⅔ and –¾ → –⁸⁄₁₂ and –⁹⁄₁₂ → –⅔ is greater.
On the number line, positive rationals are to the right of 0, negative to the left.

## Operations
- –⅖ + ⅗ = **⅕**
- ⅔ – (–¼) = ⅔ + ¼ = **¹¹⁄₁₂**
- (–⅗) × ⁴⁄₇ = **–¹²⁄₃₅**
- ⁴⁄₉ ÷ (–⅔) = ⁴⁄₉ × (–³⁄₂) = **–⅔**
> 💡 There are infinitely many rational numbers between any two rational numbers.

## Let's check
Q: Write –²⁰⁄₃₆ in standard form.
A: –⁵⁄₉.
Q: Which is greater: –½ or –⅓?
A: –⅓.
Q: –⅚ + ⅓ = ?
A: –½.

## Formulas
- **Square:** P = 4a; A = a²
- **Rectangle:** P = 2(l + b); A = l × b
- **Parallelogram:** A = base × height
- **Triangle:** A = ½ × base × height
- **Circle:** circumference C = 2πr = πd; area A = πr² (π ≈ ²²⁄₇ or 3.14)

## Examples
- Parallelogram with base 12 cm and height 7 cm → A = **84 cm²**.
- Triangle with base 10 cm, height 6 cm → A = **30 cm²**.
- Circle of radius 7 cm → C = 2 × ²²⁄₇ × 7 = **44 cm**; A = ²²⁄₇ × 7 × 7 = **154 cm²**.

## Paths and borders
A garden 20 m × 15 m has a 2 m wide path outside it. Outer rectangle = 24 m × 19 m = 456 m². Path area = 456 – 300 = **156 m²**.

## Unit conversions
1 cm² = 100 mm²; 1 m² = 10,000 cm²; 1 hectare = 10,000 m².
> 💡 A wheel of circumference 2 m covers 2 m in one full turn.

## Let's check
Q: Area of a triangle with base 14 cm and height 9 cm?
A: 63 cm².
Q: Circumference of a circle with diameter 21 cm?
A: 66 cm.
Q: Area of a circle of radius 14 cm?
A: 616 cm².

## Terms, factors and coefficients
In the expression **5x² – 3xy + 7**:
- **Terms:** 5x², –3xy, 7
- **Coefficient** of x² is 5; of xy is –3
- **Constant term:** 7
- **Like terms** have the same variables and powers (3a and –8a); **unlike terms** don't (3a and 3b).

## Types of expressions
- **Monomial:** one term – 7xy
- **Binomial:** two terms – a + 5
- **Trinomial:** three terms – x² + 2x + 1
- **Polynomial:** one or more terms

## Adding and subtracting
Combine like terms only:
(3x² + 5x – 2) + (2x² – 7x + 9) = **5x² – 2x + 7**
(6a – 4b) – (2a + 3b) = 6a – 4b – 2a – 3b = **4a – 7b**

## Finding the value
If x = 2, then 3x² – 4x + 1 = 3(4) – 8 + 1 = **5**.
> 💡 When subtracting, change the sign of every term in the bracket being subtracted.

## Let's check
Q: What is the coefficient of y in –9xy?
A: –9x.
Q: Add: 4m + 3n and 2m – 5n.
A: 6m – 2n.
Q: Find the value of a² + 2a when a = –3.
A: 3.

## Exponents
**aⁿ** means a multiplied by itself n times. 2⁵ = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 32. Here 2 is the **base** and 5 is the **exponent (power)**.

## Laws of exponents
- aᵐ × aⁿ = **aᵐ⁺ⁿ** → 2³ × 2⁴ = 2⁷
- aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = **aᵐ⁻ⁿ** → 5⁶ ÷ 5² = 5⁴
- (aᵐ)ⁿ = **aᵐⁿ** → (3²)³ = 3⁶
- aᵐ × bᵐ = **(ab)ᵐ** → 2³ × 5³ = 10³
- **a⁰ = 1** (a ≠ 0)
- (–1)^even = 1; (–1)^odd = –1

## Standard form (scientific notation)
Write big numbers as **k × 10ⁿ**, where 1 ≤ k < 10.
- 5,90,00,000 = **5.9 × 10⁷**
- Distance from Earth to Sun ≈ 150,000,000 km = **1.5 × 10⁸ km**
> 💡 Prime factor numbers using exponents: 72 = 2³ × 3².

## Let's check
Q: Simplify 7⁵ ÷ 7³.
A: 7² = 49.
Q: What is (–1)¹⁰¹?
A: –1.
Q: Write 3,40,000 in standard form.
A: 3.4 × 10⁵.

## Line symmetry review
A figure has line symmetry if it can be folded along a line so the two halves match. Regular polygons have as many lines of symmetry as sides.

## Rotational symmetry
A figure has **rotational symmetry** if it looks exactly the same after a rotation of **less than 360°** about a fixed point (the **centre of rotation**).
- **Angle of rotation:** the smallest angle that brings it back to the same look.
- **Order of rotational symmetry:** how many times it looks the same in a full turn.

## Examples
- Square: order **4** (every 90°)
- Equilateral triangle: order **3** (every 120°)
- Rectangle: order **2** (every 180°)
- Circle: infinite order
- Letter **S**, **Z**, **N**: order 2 (no line symmetry!)
- A windmill with four blades: order 4

## Both kinds
A square has 4 lines of symmetry and rotational symmetry of order 4. Letters H, I, O, X have both.
> 💡 Order 1 means the figure only matches itself after a full 360° turn – it has no rotational symmetry.

## Let's check
Q: What is the order of rotational symmetry of a square?
A: 4.
Q: Does the letter Z have line symmetry?
A: No (it has rotational symmetry of order 2).
Q: What is the angle of rotation for an equilateral triangle?
A: 120°.

## 2-D and 3-D shapes
- **2-D (plane) shapes:** length and breadth – square, circle, triangle.
- **3-D (solid) shapes:** length, breadth and height – cube, cuboid, cylinder, cone, sphere, pyramid.

## Faces, edges and vertices
- **Cube/cuboid:** 6 faces, 12 edges, 8 vertices
- **Square pyramid:** 5 faces, 8 edges, 5 vertices
- **Triangular pyramid (tetrahedron):** 4 faces, 6 edges, 4 vertices
- **Triangular prism:** 5 faces, 9 edges, 6 vertices
**Euler's formula:** F + V – E = 2 → for a cube, 6 + 8 – 12 = 2 ✔

## Nets
A **net** is a flat shape that folds into a solid. A cube's net has 6 squares joined in a certain way.

## Views
A solid looks different from the **front**, **side** and **top**. A cylinder's top view is a circle; its front view is a rectangle.

## Cross-sections
Slicing a solid gives a cross-section: a cube sliced straight gives a square; a cylinder sliced across gives a circle.
> 💡 Isometric dot paper helps us draw 3-D shapes on paper.

## Let's check
Q: How many edges does a triangular prism have?
A: 9.
Q: Verify Euler's formula for a square pyramid.
A: F + V – E = 5 + 5 – 8 = 2 ✔
Q: What is the top view of a cone?
A: A circle (with a point at the centre).
